Design the questionnaire for evidence, not essays

A 40-page generic questionnaire teaches suppliers to write fiction. Ask for artifacts instead: their org chart, a sample CoA for the exact material, the calibration record for the instrument behind that CoA, their last two years of recalls/complaints, insurance certificate, three references shipping similar volume. Ten evidence requests outperform a hundred yes/no boxes, they take the supplier less time, and the refusals are as informative as the answers. For biological suppliers add the specific ones: APHIS/CDC permits held (L4), donor/consent documentation practice, cold-chain packout qualification.

What each gate is actually for

The NDA gate protects both sides before real information moves (and is your own supplier-IP discipline — Stage 2.3). The questionnaire screens out the clearly unfit cheaply. The assessment (desk or site) verifies the paper matches reality — on a site visit, your DC-walking instincts transfer directly: material flow, segregation, labeling, housekeeping, and whether the floor matches the SOPs. The trial lot answers the only question that matters — does the material work in your process — under written acceptance criteria agreed before the lot ships, so "did it pass?" is a lookup, not a debate. And ASL entry records scope: approved for what (material, grade, site) — a supplier approved for research-grade gelatin is not approved for a new material by vibes.

The quality agreement, clause by clause

One to three pages, plain language, signed by both: (1) the spec by revision number, and who controls changes to it; (2) CoA content per lot — the parameters, methods, and units you'll trend (N2 depends on this clause); (3) change notification — material, process, site, or sub-supplier changes require written notice N days before shipment (the silent-change problem is the classic killer at companies without a mandated QMS); (4) minimum remaining dating on arrival (L6's math depends on it); (5) records retention and your audit rights (site or remote, notice period); (6) nonconformance handling — response time to a SCAR, return logistics, who pays freight on rejects; (7) flow-down — your customers' relevant requirements passed through. Every clause exists because some week you'll need it; write them while the relationship is warm.

Conditional approvals and honest exits

Real life needs material before gates finish. The professional tool is conditional approval: approved for N lots or M months, with named conditions (100% incoming inspection, single study only) and an expiry date that forces the decision. The other honest tool is disqualification: a written rule for what removes a supplier from the ASL (failed requalification, unresolved SCAR, ethics breach) — because a list nobody can leave isn't a controlled list, it's a phone book.

Requalification triggers, not anniversaries

Annual requalification of everyone is theater at your scale. Trigger-based is stronger and cheaper: a change notification, a major or repeat NCR (L1), an audit finding, a financial-health flag (N4's KRI), two years elapsed for HIGH-tier suppliers. Write the triggers into the procedure and the system runs itself — which is the whole trick of a one-person quality function.

Shewhart's insight, and why tampering backfires

Walter Shewhart's 1920s insight still runs every serious factory: a stable process varies, and treating that variation as a series of problems makes it worse. Deming's funnel experiment proves it — adjust a stable process after every observation and you roughly double its spread. In procurement terms: renegotiating after every price wiggle, emailing the supplier after every slightly-low CoA, re-planning after every late truck — all tampering. The chart's gift is a decision rule for when to act, which is also a license for when not to. For a one-person function, the license matters as much as the alarm.

I-MR: the chart for your actual data

Classic Xbar-R charts assume subgroups (5 parts per hour). Your reality is one CoA value per lot — the Individuals chart's home turf. Limits come from the moving range (the lot-to-lot jumps): sigma ≈ average moving range ÷ 1.128, then CL ±3σ. Two practical rules: compute limits from a stable baseline (the first 15–20 lots that look boring) and freeze them — limits that re-compute every lot chase the drift they should expose; recompute only after a verified process change (the supplier fixed something, N1's change notification fired). Minitab does this in two clicks; Excel does it in four columns; the thinking is what you're learning.

The run rules, and why they exist

A ±3σ breach catches big shifts fast but small drifts slowly. The supplementary rules (Western Electric being the classic set) trade a little false-alarm rate for early warning: 8 consecutive points on one side of the CL (a shifted mean), 6 in a row rising or falling (a trend — your red points), 2 of 3 beyond 2σ on the same side (a shift starting). Pick two or three rules and write them into your CoA-review SOP; a rule set nobody agreed on becomes "I don't like this chart," which is tampering with extra steps.

Capability: the one-number bridge to specs

Once stable, ask how comfortably the process fits the spec: Cp = (USL−LSL)/6σ (potential, if centered), Cpk = distance from mean to nearest spec limit ÷ 3σ (reality). This chart's baseline: σ≈2.3, spec width 20 → Cp≈1.44; mean 249.4 vs nearest limit 240 → Cpk≈1.36 — decent, not luxurious, and visibly eroding as the trend runs. Capability numbers are how you set L8's inspection intensity rationally: a Cpk-2 supplier earns skip-lot; a Cpk-1 supplier earns every-lot attention — inspection effort priced by evidence.

The supplier conversation a chart enables

Sharing the chart changes the relationship's grammar. "Your last four lots trend up" with a picture is collaborative diagnosis; "your material is getting worse" without one is an accusation. Mature suppliers often start sending their own charts once they see yours — at which point you're reviewing their process control instead of policing their outputs, which is the entire maturity ladder of supplier quality in one sentence. And when a supplier's CoA values never vary at all: that's not excellence, that's a copy-paste — a finding worth a very polite audit question (N1's audit rights clause).

Building each line without their data

Materials: their BOM is unknown, but chemistry and physics are public — input commodity prices (trade data, index series, distributor list prices), times quantity, divided by realistic yield. Your Smithfield yield instincts price this line better than most consultants. Conversion: estimate batch size, cycle time, and crew from what you saw or can ask, times a loaded regional labor rate (BLS publishes them). This is a time study done from the parking lot — your core craft. Overhead: don't agonize; use a defensible band (small specialty plants commonly run 80–200% of direct labor) and carry it as a stated assumption. SG&A and margin: industry norms — 8–15% each for small specialty manufacturers; public comparables' financials calibrate it. Write every assumption next to its number; the model's credibility is its visible humility.

Using it at the table without starting a war

Never present a should-cost as an accusation ("you're overcharging by $42"). Present it as your homework and an invitation: "Here's how I understand your cost structure — what am I missing?" Three good outcomes follow: they correct you (you learn their real structure — valuable), they justify the gap (small-batch penalty, special handling — now those are negotiable line items), or they move. All three beat arguing about one opaque number. This is L10's "objective criteria" doctrine with teeth: the model is the criterion you brought.

The levers the model reveals

Once costs have lines, price has levers. Materials-heavy? Indexation (N6) protects both sides, and spec tolerance review (L2's quadrant-move) may open cheaper inputs. Labor/batch-heavy? Larger release quantities against a blanket PO (L9) directly attack their small-batch penalty — propose the price step yourself: "what does the 25 kg batch price look like?" Learning curve: for a young supplier scaling with you, agree stepped pricing tied to cumulative volume — you're literally scheduling their learning curve into the contract. Each lever converts "please be cheaper" into a mechanism both sides can say yes to.

Open-book: the end state, rarely the start

With true strategic partners, cost transparency can be contractual: actual costs shared, margin agreed, both sides attack cost together (the gainshare logic your Smithfield vendor-SLA instincts already know). It requires trust, an agreed accounting method, and something in it for them (volume security, co-investment). Don't ask a new supplier for open-book; earn it, or approximate it forever with a good model — an 80% model you own beats a 100% disclosure you'll never get.

Common failure modes

Zeroing the margin line (a supplier priced to survive is a supply risk you built yourself — N4 will flag them); false precision ($137.42 invites ridicule; $135–145 invites discussion); modeling their list process rather than their actual one (ask before assuming automation levels); and forgetting your own switching costs while wielding the model (L7) — a $42 gap may still be worth paying against a six-month requalification.

Why cause → event → impact is non-negotiable

Vague risks get vague treatments. "Supplier risk: high" invites hand-wringing; "thin financials (cause) → supplier exits (event) → 6-month gap (impact)" invites exactly three interventions — watch the cause (financial KRI), pre-empt the event (second source), or shrink the impact (buffer). The sentence structure is the analysis: writing it forces you to know which link you're treating, and reviewers can disagree usefully with a link where they could only nod at an adjective.

Scores: coarse, honest, and never multiplied into false precision

Hubbard's critique (you'll meet it again in Stage 4) lands on two abuses: range compression (everything scores 3–4, so ranking is noise) and treating ordinal products as math (a 3×5 is not "the same risk" as a 5×3 — one is a rare catastrophe, the other a chronic bleed, and they need different treatments). Defenses: anchor the scales in words (Impact 5 = "a study is lost or a customer commitment is missed"; Likelihood 4 = "expected within a year"), keep 1–5 coarse, and let severity dominate ties — the same severity-first instinct the AIAG-VDA FMEA (L5) already taught you. The register ranks attention; Stage 4's expected-dollar modeling ranks money. Don't ask the coarse tool to do the fine tool's job.

Inherent vs. residual: the two-column trick that funds controls

Score each risk twice — before controls and after. R-02 inherent (3×4) drops to residual (2×4) because of the shipping SOP and loggers. That delta is the plainest justification of quality spend a leadership team ever sees: "these controls move four risks from red to amber; removing this budget moves them back." It's COPQ logic (L1) drawn as arrows, and it's why the two-column register outperforms every eloquent memo about "being careful."

Choosing KRIs that actually lead

A good KRI is observable from data you already touch, cheap to compute, and moves before the event. From your world: lead-time creep percentage (L9's GR data — quiet capacity stress at the supplier), CoA drift (N2's chart — a run rule trip IS a KRI firing), D&B or payment-behavior changes (financial stress precedes exits by quarters), committed-vs-requested allocation ratio (donor-network tightness), freezer alarm frequency (storage risk building). Two disciplines: one KRI per risk at most (a dashboard of twenty "risk indicators" is a KPI report wearing a costume), and a written trigger-response ("crosses +15% → requalification conversation within 2 weeks") — a KRI without a pre-agreed response is a smoke alarm nobody agreed to leave the building for.

Running it: fifteen minutes a month, plus triggers

The cadence that survives contact with a real calendar: monthly, 15 minutes, three questions per top row — did the KRI move? did the treatment advance? does the score still fit? Plus event triggers (a SCAR, a change notice, a KRI breach) that pull a row's review forward. Present upward as top-5 + asks, never the whole register: leadership funds specific mitigations attached to specific risks; nobody funds a spreadsheet. And date everything — a register whose "next review" column is in the past is visibly dead, which is exactly the honesty a good system enforces on its owner.

The mental model: GROUP BY answers about groups, windows answer about rows

"Average OTD per supplier" is a GROUP BY question — one row per supplier, detail gone. "Each receipt, with its supplier's rolling OTD at that moment" is a window question — every receipt keeps its lot number, date, and quantity, plus a computed context column. Dashboards live on the second kind: trends, bands, rankings, and deltas are all row-level questions about group-level math. Once you see the split, you'll stop exporting to Excel to "add the rolling column" — which is the moment SQL stops being a query language and becomes your analysis language.

The three window shapes you'll actually use

Rolling metrics: AVG/SUM with ORDER BY and a ROWS frame — the scorecard's moving averages, smoothing lumpy monthly OTD into a trend a human can read (and N2's chart can chart). Dedup/latest-per-key: ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY lot ORDER BY received_at DESC), keep row_num = 1 — the correct way to get "the latest CoA per lot" from a table of revisions, replacing every fragile sort-and-remove-duplicates you've ever done. Deltas: LAG(lead_time) gives last order's lead time; subtract for creep; three such lines and N4's KRI is a live query instead of a quarterly chore. Between these three shapes you can rebuild ~90% of LMS-Light's calculation layer.

CTEs: pipelines you can read at 7 a.m.

WITH clean AS (fix types, filter cancelled orders), scored AS (add on_time CASE), rolled AS (add the window) SELECT … — each step named, testable alone (SELECT * FROM clean), and reused without repetition. It's the applied-steps pane made portable, and it's also the difference between SQL a colleague can maintain and SQL that dies with its author. Habit worth forming now: one transformation per CTE, named for what comes out of it.

One honest gotcha

Window functions run after WHERE (remember L11's execution order) — so you can't filter on a window result directly. The idiom: compute the window in a CTE, filter in the next step. The error message you'd otherwise meet is cryptic; the pattern makes it never appear.

Finding the right series (the skill inside the skill)

Writing the clause so it protects both sides

A workable escalation clause has six named parts: the formula (new price = base price × Σ share_i × index_i change), the index series by ID, the base month, the adjustment frequency (annual is sane; quarterly invites churn), a cap and collar (e.g., ±6% per year — protects you from spikes, them from collapses), and symmetry — the same formula runs down. The forgotten symmetry clause is where buyers lose quietly: suppliers propose indexation in rising markets and go silent in falling ones. Signed in a calm year, the clause converts every future price letter from a negotiation into a verification — which, for a sole-source relationship you can't walk away from, is often the single most valuable contract term available to you (worth more than the last percent of this year's price, and a classic L10 trade: accept indexation, get capacity reservation).

When indexation is the wrong tool

In genuinely competitive categories (L2's leverage quadrant), indexation locks in increases that competition would have negotiated away — just re-bid instead. And a formula can't price real structural change: a supplier who re-tooled, changed batch sizes, or lost their own supplier needs an N3 conversation, not an index lookup. The tools stack: Kraljic decides whether to formula or to compete; should-cost decides whether the claimed structure is real; the index audits the movement within it.

Every failure has two root causes

The mistake most teams make is stopping at "why did the supplier ship bad material?" A mature loop always asks a second question: "why didn't our controls catch it before it mattered?" That second cause is the escape point — and fixing it is usually cheaper and more in your control than fixing the supplier. If the 210-Bloom lot reached the lab bench before anyone read the CoA, the escape is your intake process, and no SCAR fixes that.

The 8D disciplines, and what "good" looks like at each

D1 team — even at a supplier of 12 people, ask who was on it; a one-name 8D is a form, not an investigation. D2 problem description — quantified (what, where, when, how many, how detected); vague D2s predict vague root causes. D3 containment — both sides: they quarantine their stock and in-process work, you quarantine yours. D4 root cause — demand evidence, not opinion ("we retrained the operator" without a cause analysis is the classic non-answer). D5/D6 chosen and implemented corrective action — dated, owned, verifiable. D7 prevent recurrence — the fix applied system-wide (other products, other lines), which separates good suppliers from box-checkers. D8 recognition — skip it externally, but note internally which suppliers investigate honestly: that's qualification gold.

Interim vs. permanent, and verification with teeth

Accept an ICA (100% inspection at their dock, say) to keep supply moving, but never let it silently become permanent — log a due date for the PCA. Verification of effectiveness needs a defined method and window before you close: "next 5 lots' CoAs show in-process Bloom results" is verifiable; "supplier says it's fixed" is not. A CAPA closed without verification is the most common audit finding in every industry.

When NOT to issue a SCAR

SCARs are a currency; overspend it and suppliers stop taking it seriously (and start hiding problems). Reserve formal SCARs for safety/major impact, repeats, or systemic smells. Everything else goes in the NCR log as trend data with a defined trigger — e.g., three minor NCRs from one supplier in a quarter escalates to a SCAR automatically. Written triggers protect the relationship: it's the rule escalating, not your mood.

COPQ — pricing the loop for executives

Quality costs split into prevention (qualification, audits), appraisal (inspection, CoA review), internal failure (scrap, rework, retest), and external failure (a bad model reaching a customer — by far the most expensive). The old 1–10–100 rule of thumb: a dollar of prevention saves ten of appraisal and a hundred of failure. When you ask leadership for audit time or incoming-check equipment, frame it in COPQ — it's the same capital-justification language as your $50M portfolio, pointed at quality.

Even a three-person MRB matters

Disposition authority should never be one person — not because you're untrustworthy, but because "use-as-is" decisions need R&D's science and someone accountable for the customer consequence. A lightweight MRB at your size: you + a scientist + whoever owns the study. Put it in the procedure; a customer auditor will ask who can accept nonconforming material, and "Oscar decides alone" is the wrong answer even when Oscar decides well.

Scoring the axes honestly (a working rubric)

The matrix is only as good as its inputs, so score 1–5 on each axis instead of eyeballing. The strategies are verbs, not labels

Routine: consolidate suppliers, catalog/punch-out ordering, blanket POs, p-card, never spend meeting time. Leverage: run real competitions (RFQs, L-Sprint-2), switch without sentiment, push payment terms. Bottleneck: buffer within shelf life (L6), pre-qualify alternates before you need them, monitor supplier health, accept paying a premium for certainty — price pressure here is actively dangerous. Strategic: LTAs with capacity reservation, joint forecasting, top-to-top relationships, and supplier development (your kaizen skill) — you invest in these suppliers like internal assets.

Moving items between quadrants is the real game

The matrix isn't static — the best category managers move items deliberately. Bottleneck → routine: standardize the spec so more suppliers qualify (is 250 Bloom truly required, or would 240–260 open three more makers?). Where supplier power actually comes from

Concentration is only the loudest source. The quieter ones: differentiation (their tissue-prep protocol is validated into your product — that's power they gained the day your validation finished); your switching costs (requalification months are a moat they didn't have to build); your share of their revenue (a supplier for whom you're 0.5% of sales can afford to lose you — one at 15% cannot; estimate this number for every critical supplier, it's the single best one-number power gauge); and information asymmetry (they know their costs, you don't — which is exactly what should-cost modeling attacks in Sprint 2).

The preferencing matrix, used deliberately

Suppliers run the same portfolio logic you do. Core: big and pleasant — gets the best people, allocation priority, early warning of problems. Development: small but promising — gets investment because of what the account could become. Exploitable: big but painful — served at a premium with minimum effort. Nuisance: small and painful — last in every queue. You cannot change your size this year, so the levers are all on the "pleasant/promising" axis: pay exactly on time (be famous for it), send clean POs and forecasts (L9's machinery), make their logistics easy, and tell the story — a novel ex-vivo application riding the FDA's animal-testing-reduction wave is genuinely interesting, and niche suppliers fund interesting. Ask your rep outright: "what would make us a better customer for you?" The answer is a free map of their preferencing criteria.

Small-buyer price mechanics

List price is the price for anonymous nuisance accounts. Distributors and manufacturers run tiers (volume, contract, institutional); a modest annual commitment often unlocks a tier your monthly ordering never will — the volume is identical, only the promise changed (that's L10's "trade certainty for terms" in its simplest form). Buying groups and consortium purchasing (common among labs and research institutions) are the structural version: aggregated demand converts many nuisance accounts into one core account. Worth one exploratory call.

When the answer isn't negotiation at all

Against a true monopoly with rising prices, the economic answers are structural: qualify a substitute (change the input), redesign the spec (escape the differentiation moat), integrate (make it yourself — L7's make-vs-buy with a power motive), or buffer and wait (new entrants come to profitable markets). Recognizing which structure you face keeps you from spending relationship capital on a negotiation that structure has already decided.

The exempt lane, done properly

"Exempt" never means "unregulated" — it means a trained person judged the material out of scope and documented why. Exempt human/animal specimens (minimal likelihood of pathogens — fixed tissues, many research materials) still require triple-style packaging and the words "Exempt human specimen" or "Exempt animal specimen" on the box. Cold without dangerous goods: the LN2 dry shipper

Excursion response — decide before, not during

The discipline that makes FMEAs useful: mode ≠ cause ≠ effect

Most bad FMEAs blur the three columns. Keep the chain mechanical: a function ("deliver gelatin at 250±10 Bloom"), its failure mode (the function negated: "Bloom below 240"), the effect downstream ("model stiffness out of spec → batch scrapped → study slips"), and the cause upstream ("no in-process Bloom verification at blending"). Severity belongs to the effect, occurrence to the cause, detection to your current controls — score the wrong column and the arithmetic is meaningless. One mode can have several causes; write them as separate rows, because they need different actions.

Prevention beats detection — and the scores say so

Every action either lowers Occurrence (prevention: fix the cause — supplier adds the in-process check, spec gets a wider tolerance, dual source) or lowers Detection's number (detection: catch it — incoming test, CoA trend review, logger in the box). Prevention is almost always the better dollar: it removes risk, while detection just intercepts it, forever, at recurring cost. When your action list is all detection, you're taxing yourself annually for a problem the supplier could delete once — that observation, made politely with the FMEA on the table, is one of the most persuasive supplier-development conversations there is.

Scoring anchors (calibrate once, reuse forever)

Write a half-page anchor table so scores mean the same thing next quarter: Severity — 10: safety/regulatory or customer study invalidated; 8: batch lost; 5: rework/delay days; 2: cosmetic. Occurrence — tie to data where you have it (your statistical strength): 8: seen quarterly or no control exists; 5: seen yearly; 2: never seen in 3+ years with a control in place. Detection — 10: no control could catch it before use; 7: catchable only by chance; 4: systematic check catches most; 1: automated/100% verification. Anchored scores are also defensible in front of a customer auditor — unanchored ones read as vibes.

Running it as a one-person function (without it becoming fiction)

FMEA is designed as a team sport because one person's blind spots become the document's blind spots. Your lightweight version: draft rows yourself (you know the supply chain), then run a 45-minute review with one scientist (they know what severity really means for the model) and, when possible, the supplier's technical contact (they know occurrence honestly). Asking a supplier for their PFMEA is also a first-class qualification probe (Stage 2.1): mature suppliers hand you one; the silence of the others is itself data.

Keeping it alive

Define revision triggers, or the FMEA dies the week after you write it: any SCAR (L1) — did the FMEA foresee it? If not, add the row and ask what else it's missing; any spec, supplier, or process change; the annual category review (L2). An FMEA whose Occurrence scores never move despite a year of NCR data isn't a risk tool, it's wall art.

Cousins worth knowing exist

Bow-tie diagrams (one hazard, threats on the left, consequences on the right, barriers between) communicate a single big risk to executives better than any table. Fault trees work top-down from one catastrophic event with AND/OR logic. FMEA remains the workhorse because it's exhaustive and cheap — but for "the donor network fails" as a single scenario, a bow-tie one-pager (Stage 2.4's continuity work) will land harder in the boardroom.

CSL vs. fill rate — don't let the numbers lie to you

A 95% cycle service level means 1 cycle in 20 has a stockout — even a one-unit, one-day one. The fill rate — fraction of total demand served — for the same policy is usually much higher (a brief small shortage barely dents it). Executives intuitively hear "fill rate" when you say "service level," so name which one you're quoting. For critical study materials the honest target is usually a high CSL on a short list of items, not a blanket number across everything — which is what segmentation is for.

The full formula, and when you need it

With lead-time variability: SS = z·√(LT·σ_d² + d²·σ_LT²) — demand noise scaled by lead time, plus lead-time noise scaled by demand rate. The second term is the one that explodes for your suppliers: a niche maker whose "3 weeks" is really 2–6 weeks contributes more buffer requirement through σ_LT than demand noise ever will. That gives you a negotiable insight: lead-time reliability is worth more than lead-time speed — a guaranteed 4 weeks beats a nominal 2-that's-sometimes-6, and it's a cheaper concession for the supplier to give (L10 trade material). Unit discipline: σ_d and LT must share the same period (both weekly or both monthly) — mixing them is the most common spreadsheet error in this domain.

You'll actually run periodic review

Continuous (s,Q) assumes someone watches stock in real time. Small companies order on a rhythm — "every Monday" — which is (R,S): every R days, order up to level S. The honest cost: exposure now spans R + LT, so S = expected demand over (R+LT) + z·σ·√(R+LT) — slightly more buffer than continuous review needs. Batch your Monday buying consciously and the math stays honest.

Segment before you calculate

Run ABC (annual value) × XYZ (predictability) and give each cell a policy, not a formula-for-everything: AX items (high value, steady) get tight SS and frequent orders; AZ items (high value, erratic — most research-driven demand) get the full formula plus human review of upcoming studies; CX consumables get big lazy buffers because carrying cost is trivial; CZ items get "order on evidence." Ten minutes of segmentation saves you from polishing safety stock on gloves while a critical medium runs on feel.

Perishables: three rules the formula doesn't know

1) Cap by dating: max useful stock ≈ demand rate × usable shelf life on arrival (ask suppliers to commit minimum remaining dating at delivery — a quality-agreement clause most buyers forget). 2) FEFO or the math is fiction: one grab of fresh stock ahead of old and your real expiry loss doubles — shelf discipline is an SOP, not a preference. 3) Carrying cost includes death: for a 6-month-dated material, expected expiry can dwarf capital cost; charge it per unit-year when comparing "hold more" against alternatives like split deliveries (L9's blanket PO) or supplier-held stock.

A ten-column worksheet is the whole system

Item, demand/wk, σ_d, LT, σ_LT, target CSL, z, SS, ROP, shelf-life cap — one row per critical material, reviewed monthly. That sheet, versioned and dated, is simultaneously your planning system, your finance justification for inventory, and a Stage 4 S&OP input. The Incoterms ladder in one paragraph

Eleven terms, one axis: how far the seller carries cost and risk. EXW (nothing — you arrange pickup at their dock) → FCA (they hand to your carrier) → FOB/CIF (sea terms; risk passes as goods board the vessel — CIF adds their freight+insurance to the port only) → DAP (they deliver to your door, you pay import duty) → DDP (door delivery, duty included). Two traps for cold chain: under EXW/FCA you own transit risk, so a thawed box is your loss and your carrier claim; and CIF's included insurance covers to the port, not your dock. For perishables from small suppliers, DAP is usually worth paying for — the supplier who packs it also owns getting it to you cold, which aligns incentives exactly where you want them.

Duty and the tariff decade

Every import has an HTS code, and the code — not the product's story — sets the duty. Many research biologicals and reagents enter at low or zero duty, but the classification obligation is real, and lab consumables and equipment made in China frequently carry Section 301 tariffs that change with the political wind (a live issue through 2025–26). Practical hygiene: know the HTS code and duty for your top imported items (your broker will tell you), keep country-of-origin in the item master, and re-check exposure when sourcing moves. "What's our tariff exposure?" is a question leadership will eventually ask — be the person with the one-page answer.

Freight economics worth knowing by feel

Parcel (FedEx/UPS) prices by max(actual, DIM) weight — a light, bulky validated shipper bills at its volume, which is why cold-chain packaging choice changes freight, not just packaging cost. Overnight cold-chain parcel is fine to roughly a shipper's qualified hold time; past that you're into air freight + specialty courier territory where minimums run high but per-kg falls. The structural lesson: freight has step functions, so consolidation is free money — two monthly orders merged into one saves an entire fixed shipment cost, which compounds with the MOQ/expiry math from the chart.

Using Z1.4 for real (the three-step lookup)

Step 1: lot size + inspection level → code letter (General Level II is the default; Level I halves scrutiny, III raises it). Step 2: code letter → sample size n in the master table. Switching rules are codified trust

Two rejections in five consecutive lots → tightened inspection (same n, stingier Ac); five clean lots on tightened → back to normal; sustained clean history (plus stable production) earns reduced. This is the statistical spine under "trusted supplier" programs: dock-to-stock isn't a favor, it's a status earned by lot history and revocable by it. Implement even a simplified version and your inspection effort automatically flows toward the suppliers who currently deserve it — which is exactly what a one-person function needs.

Two numbers define a plan: AQL and LTPD

AQL is the producer-side promise (good lots nearly always pass). LTPD/RQL is the consumer-side promise: the defect rate that should nearly always fail (your β=10% point — ~7.5% on the chart). Any plan is a compromise between the two, and the gap between them is what your sample size buys: bigger n steepens the curve, separating good from bad more sharply. When someone asks "why are we inspecting 50?", the honest answer is "to hold α≈9% at our AQL and β≈10% at 7.5% defective" — and now you can draw it.

CoA-based acceptance, formalized (your main lane)

When testing is destructive, the assurance moves upstream in three layers: validate the measurement once (their lab's method and gage R&R — or a one-time split-sample comparison against a third-party lab); trend every certificate (plot their reported Bloom lot-over-lot — a supplier whose numbers never vary is as suspicious as one drifting toward a spec limit; this is SPC applied to CoAs, next sprint's statistical lesson); verify periodically (an occasional independent retest, frequency by risk). Add sampling only where non-destructive — documentation, weights, packaging integrity, temperature logs. Write these three layers into the quality agreement and you have an acceptance system a customer auditor will respect, built for materials you can't afford to consume.

Randomness is a procedure, not a vibe

"Grab some from the top" defeats the statistics — defects cluster (one bad shipping position, one production hour). Number the units and use a random pick (even a spreadsheet RAND) or stratify across the lot's structure (cartons, positions). It takes ninety seconds and it's the difference between an inference and a ritual.

Match variants, and choosing tolerances like an adult

Two-way (PO=Invoice) suits services with no receivable goods; three-way is the goods standard; four-way adds the inspection result — which for your critical materials means payment can wait for quality acceptance, a control worth having for expensive biologicals (write payment-on-acceptance into the terms, L10 trade material). Tolerances are a design choice with teeth: too tight and you drown in trivial exceptions (a $0.40 freight rounding blocking a payment); too loose and price creep rides through unexamined. Common sense at small scale: percentage price tolerance (±2%), small absolute floor, quantity tolerance matched to how the material is actually measured (tissue by weight will never match to the gram — set the tolerance from measurement reality, not accounting neatness).

The battle of the forms — the fine print war you're already in

Your PO says your T&Cs govern; their order acknowledgment says theirs do. Under US commercial law the last document sent before performance often wins ("last shot"), and suppliers know it — which is why acknowledgments exist. Practical defenses at your size: get an MSA or quality agreement signed once (it outranks the forms war entirely — Stage 2.3), read acknowledgments for the three terms that matter (price validity, lead time, liability/warranty), and when they conflict, reply reserving your PO terms rather than staying silent. You don't need to litigate this — you need to stop losing it by default.

PO types as planning tools

A standard PO buys a thing once. A blanket/framework PO approves a ceiling ("up to 24 kg gelatin, $5,500, 12 months") and then releases against it — one approval, many deliveries, which is how L6's split-delivery answer and L7's consolidation answer become administratively free. Releases also give suppliers a demand signal without new paperwork — a customer-of-choice behavior (L3) that costs you nothing. Contract-referencing POs go one step further: the PO carries only quantity and date; the LTA carries everything else.

Why finance keeps asking about open POs

Every GR without an invoice sits in the GR/IR clearing account, and month-end accruals are built from it — so stale open POs and unposted receipts literally distort the company's books. Being the buyer who closes POs, posts GRs same-day, and keeps the open-order report clean makes finance an ally, and at a small company the buyer with clean books gets believed when the same spreadsheets ask for budget (Stage 3's credibility, banked early).

The sole-buyer control kit, concretely

You can't split yourself into three people, so compensate structurally: a written DOA (above $X, a second signature — even the CEO's — approves the PR); someone else posts GR (whoever physically receives — lab staff — clicks "received," which is both a control and honest data); monthly exception review witnessed by finance or your manager (fifteen minutes over the match-exception and open-PO reports, initialed); no self-dealing rules written down (gifts, conflicts declared — Stage 2's ethics policy). Fraud patterns these defeat are depressingly standard: ghost vendors, split POs ducking under approval thresholds, duplicate invoices — auditors look for exactly these, and finding the controls already written moves you from "finding" to "commendation."

1 · The Harvard core: four ideas that carry everything

Separate the people from the problem — be soft on the person, hard on the issue; a supplier rep who feels attacked defends, one who feels respected problem-solves. Negotiate interests, not positions — the classic tale: two people fight over an orange, split it, and each gets half of what they needed; one wanted the fruit, the other the peel. Their +12% is a position; the interests underneath might be covering a real input-cost spike, hitting a revenue target, or derisking a big new customer — each interest is answerable by a different (cheaper) concession than price. Invent options before deciding — the tradeables table exists so the meeting has raw material beyond one number. Insist on objective criteria — your PPI anchor isn't a tactic, it's a philosophy: let the published index argue so neither of you has to win by stubbornness. When they say 12 and the index says 3.1, the burden of explaining the gap has quietly moved to them.

2 · BATNA work is done before the meeting, in the world

Your BATNA is not a feeling of options — it's the specific, costed thing you would do Monday if talks died: qualify the alternate (6 months, $X, known risk), substitute the material (R&D says maybe), redesign the spec to open the market (L2's quadrant move), or absorb and buffer (L6's inventory literally purchases negotiating time — walking in with 10 weeks of stock changes your voice more than any technique). Improving the BATNA beats rehearsing lines: even a half-qualified second source — samples tested, audit scheduled — moves your reservation point, and suppliers can smell the difference between a buyer with options and a buyer with hopes. Then run the WATNA check honestly: if the alternate fails qualification and the buffer runs out mid-study, what actually happens? If the WATNA is catastrophic, your reservation point must be humbler than your pride wants — better to know that in the office than discover it at the table.

3 · Estimate THEIR side (you know more than you think)

Their reservation point is invisible but bounded by things you can observe: capacity utilization (a maker running at 60% needs your volume more than one at 95% — you assess capacity professionally; use that skill here), their calendar (quarter-end and fiscal-year-end make sales teams flexible), concentration (if you're 10% of their revenue, losing you is a board conversation), switching friction on their side (onboarding a replacement customer costs them too), and how much they value the reference ("supplier to the ex-vivo lab pushing NAMs" is marketing for a niche maker). Write your estimate down before the meeting and grade it after — your calibration improves fast, and the file becomes institutional memory.

4 · The one-page preparation sheet (the actual deliverable)

Ten minutes, six blocks: (1) my BATNA, costed, and its current readiness; (2) my reservation point, in numbers, derived from it; (3) my target — ambitious but explainable; (4) their estimated reservation and interests; (5) the issues table — every negotiable dimension (price, volume commitment, forecast sharing, payment terms, capacity reservation, lead-time guarantee, minimum dating on arrival, lot-consistency clause, quality-agreement terms, reference rights) with value-to-me and value-to-them columns; the asymmetries are the deal; (6) my opening — first number with its justification, or the calibrated question that starts discovery. A negotiation you can't fit on this page isn't prepared; one you can is mostly already won.

5 · At the table: sequence and craft

Open with discovery, not numbers, when you lack information: "Walk me through what's driving the increase" — every sentence of their answer prices their interests. Anchor first only with data; when they anchor first, never counter immediately (a fast counter validates their range) — label it ("12 feels like a big move for a year when your input index shows 3") and return to criteria. Trade, never concede: every move is conditional — "If you can hold 4%, then I can commit the full year" — because unconditional concessions teach the other side that waiting works. Shrink your steps: 8 → 5.5 → 4.8 → 4.5 tells a story of an approaching limit; equal steps invite infinite asking. Use MESOs when stuck: offer two packages equal in value to you (higher price + guaranteed capacity + dating commitment, vs. lower price + relaxed lead time + smaller commitment) — whichever they pick, you learn what they value, and the conversation becomes choosing rather than fighting. Deploy silence — after your number, stop talking; discomfort negotiates for you. And calibrated questions do your hardest arguing politely: "How am I supposed to take a 12% increase to leadership when the index reads 3.1?" hands them your constraint as their problem.

6 · The small-buyer playbook (your permanent situation)

Your endowment is asymmetry: certainty, predictability, and ease are nearly free for you and genuinely valuable to a small supplier — price them like assets, not gestures. Negotiate the process too: reps often can't approve structural terms, so ask early "who would need to be comfortable with a capacity reservation?" — escalating to an owner at a 30-person maker is a feature (owners think in relationships and years; reps think in quotas and quarters). Watch timing: their fiscal calendar, and your world's donor/seasonal cycles — renegotiating tissue supply during the scarce season is negotiating hungry. And remember the account-quality flywheel (L3): every on-time payment and clean forecast between negotiations is pre-negotiation — by the time you sit down, most of the outcome is already priced into how much they want to keep you.

7 · Recognize hardball; don't play it

The standard tricks, so they don't work on you: good cop/bad cop (respond to the position, not the theater), the nibble ("one small thing" after agreement — answer with a trade: "sure, if…"), phantom authority ("my manager won't accept" — then let's schedule with your manager), exploding offers (artificial deadlines — test them once; they usually reflate), the flinch (theatrical shock at your number — information-free; restate your criteria). You'll face these occasionally; don't return them. You meet the same handful of niche suppliers for years — in repeated games, a reputation for being firm, fair, and predictable outperforms every trick in this list, and it's also simply who you want to be at the table.

8 · The five classic mistakes

Deciding your reservation point during the meeting (adrenaline is a terrible economist); fighting single-issue price battles when ten issues were tradeable; revealing a weak BATNA unprompted ("we have no other option right now" — never say this, and if it's true, go build even a partial one first); treating their first offer as information about their limit (it's information about their opening theater); and skipping the debrief — five minutes logging what they valued, what worked, and your calibration error compounds into a supplier-specific playbook nobody can take from you.

The JOIN family, in receiving-dock terms

INNER JOIN keeps only matches (receipts that have a known supplier). LEFT JOIN keeps every row of the left table, matched or not — receipts with an unknown supplier_id survive with NULLs in the supplier columns, which is exactly how you find master-data problems instead of silently dropping them. RIGHT is LEFT with the tables swapped; FULL keeps everything from both. Power Query's default merge is the left outer — you've done ten thousand of these. The professional habit: after a join, sanity-check the row count; a count that grew means your "unique" key wasn't (duplicate supplier rows), and that one habit prevents most silently-wrong dashboards ever built.

NULL will bite you exactly once — make it today

NULL means "unknown," and unknown poisons comparisons: WHERE qty <> 10 quietly excludes rows where qty is NULL (unknown isn't "not 10" — it's unknown). Test with IS NULL / IS NOT NULL; wrap with COALESCE(x, 0) when a default makes sense; and know that aggregates skip NULLs (AVG of {10, NULL, 20} is 15, not 10). Your OTD query already handles this correctly by construction — the CASE decides 1 or 0 explicitly.

Why your alias fails in WHERE: the real execution order

SQL runs FROM → JOIN → WHERE → GROUP BY → HAVING → SELECT → ORDER BY — the SELECT line executes nearly last, which explains the two classic beginner errors in one stroke: you can't use otd_rate (born in SELECT) inside WHERE (which ran earlier) — filter aggregates with HAVING instead (HAVING COUNT(*) >= 5 to hide suppliers with too few receipts to judge); and WHERE filters rows before grouping while HAVING filters groups after — both useful, different jobs. Read any confusing query in execution order and it usually stops being confusing.

The suppliers table's id (primary key) and receipts' supplier_id (foreign key) are the SAP master-data hygiene you already practice, formalized. The design instinct to carry forward: every entity (supplier, item, lot, receipt) gets one table and one stable ID, and everything else references the ID — never the name (names get re-spelled; IDs don't). This is exactly the supplier/item master with lot-level CoA traceability that Stage 3.3 builds; today's lesson is its language.
