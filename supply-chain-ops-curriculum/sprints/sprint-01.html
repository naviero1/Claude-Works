L1The supplier quality loop: NCR → 8D → SCAR → CAPASupplier Engineering

The idea

When bad material arrives, professionals don't just complain to the supplier — they run a standard loop that turns one failure into a permanent fix. Every regulated company runs some version of it; you'll build yours.

The concepts

NCR (Nonconformance Report): the written record that something didn't meet spec — what, which lot, how found. If it isn't written down, it never happened. Containment: immediately fence off the suspect material (quarantine shelf, hold label) so nobody uses it while you investigate. Disposition: the decision about the bad material — use-as-is (deviation approved), rework, return, or scrap. 8D (Eight Disciplines): a standard problem-solving report format suppliers everywhere recognize: D1 team, D2 problem description, D3 containment, D4 root cause, D5 chosen corrective action, D6 implement, D7 prevent recurrence system-wide, D8 recognize the team. Your Apollo RCA skill is D4 — the hard part you already master; 8D just wraps it in a standard envelope. SCAR (Supplier Corrective Action Request): the formal letter asking a supplier to run an 8D and report back — issued for serious or repeat problems, not every hiccup. CAPA (Corrective and Preventive Action): the umbrella system tracking all fixes to closure, including verification of effectiveness: checking weeks later that the fix actually worked. COPQ (Cost of Poor Quality): what failures cost in dollars — your language for prioritizing.

Case — your worldA gelatin lot arrives and the CoA (Certificate of Analysis) shows 210 Bloom instead of the 250 spec. NCR opened; lot quarantined (containment); R&D judges it unusable for the stiffness the model needs → disposition: return. Second occurrence in 3 months → SCAR issued; the supplier's 8D finds their blending step lacks an in-process Bloom check; they add one (D7). You re-check their next 5 CoAs — effectiveness verified. Loop closed.
10-minute taskOpen a blank sheet and design your NCR log columns (date, item, lot, supplier, problem, containment, disposition, SCAR? Y/N, status, closed date). That sheet is the seed of your company's quality system.

L2The Kraljic matrix: your supply base on one 2×2Procurement

The idea

In 1983 Peter Kraljic wrote the most-used framework in procurement: plot everything you buy on two axes — profit/business impact (how much it matters) and supply risk (how hard it is to get) — and the quadrant tells you the strategy. It's the anchor your "all over the place" role is missing: it tells you where to spend relationship effort, where to automate, where to de-risk.

The four quadrants

Routine / non-critical (low impact, low risk — gloves, standard plasticware): minimize effort; catalogs, blanket POs, don't negotiate pennies. Leverage (high impact, low risk — commodity items with many suppliers): here the buyer holds power; bid competitively, switch freely. Bottleneck (low impact, high risk — a cheap adhesive only one company makes): the sneaky quadrant where cheap items stop your line; strategy is secure supply — buffer stock, alternates, monitoring — not price. Strategic (high impact, high risk — your donor-network tissue, custom biologicals): few suppliers, high stakes; strategy is partnership — long-term agreements, joint planning, executive relationships, and treating the supplier as an extension of your company.

Case — your worldFrom your own supplier shortlist: fish gelatin 250 Bloom with one strong US manufacturer (Norland) and thin alternatives is bottleneck drifting toward strategic — the strategy is qualifying that second source (which you're already doing) plus a modest buffer, not price pressure. The Sigma-Aldrich/Thermo catalog items on your ASL are routine: automate them and spend zero relationship energy there.

L3Supplier market power — and how they see YOUR accountEconomics

The idea

Prices aren't set by fairness; they're set by market structure — how many sellers there are and how easily buyers can switch. Economics gives you the diagnosis; the diagnosis picks the tactic.

The concepts

The spectrum runs from perfect competition (many sellers, identical product, nobody sets prices — think office paper) to oligopoly (a few sellers who watch each other — lab instruments) to monopoly (one seller sets terms — a patented reagent, or the only USDA-permitted tissue recovery network in your region). Monopsony is the mirror: one powerful buyer (you are not one — you're a small buyer, which shapes everything). Switching costs are what it costs you to change suppliers (re-qualification, re-validation, risk) — the higher they are, the more power your current supplier holds over you even if alternatives exist; economists call being exploited through switching costs the hold-up problem. Finally, supplier preferencing flips the lens: suppliers rank accounts too — roughly core (big, attractive), development (small but promising), exploit (big but annoying — they'll serve you at a premium), nuisance (small and annoying — you get the leftovers). A small ex-vivo company is naturally in the bottom half; the escape is being a customer of choice: easy to serve, fast-paying, forecast-sharing, interesting (your novel application is genuinely interesting to niche suppliers).

Case — your worldYour tissue source holds near-monopoly power locally — price pressure is pointless and can hurt you. But you can move from "nuisance" to "development" in their eyes: share a 6-month forecast, pay on time, be the easiest pickup on their route. That earns allocation priority in a shortage — worth more than any discount.
10-minute taskFor your top 3 suppliers write two words each: the market structure you face (monopoly / oligopoly / competitive) and, honestly, which box you occupy in their account ranking. Any "monopoly + nuisance" pair is your most urgent relationship project.

L4Shipping biological materials legally: UN3373, dry ice, triple packagingWarehouse & Logistics

The idea

Biological shipments are regulated dangerous goods. Misclassify or mispack one and the carrier rejects it (product dies in a depot), or worse, you create legal exposure. The rules are learnable in a week, the required training takes days, and knowing them makes you the site authority.

The concepts

Category A (UN2814 human/UN2900 animal): infectious substances capable of causing permanent disability or fatal disease in healthy people — packed under instruction P620, heavily restricted. Category B (UN3373, "Biological Substance, Category B"): infectious but not meeting the Category A threshold — most diagnostic and research specimens; packed under P650. Many research-grade, non-infectious materials are exempt — but that judgment must be made by a trained person and documented. Triple packaging is the universal philosophy: a leakproof primary receptacle, a leakproof secondary with absorbent, and a rigid outer — so any single failure doesn't release contents. Dry ice (UN1845) is itself a Class 9 dangerous good (it sublimates to CO₂ and can burst sealed containers or asphyxiate) with its own marking and a vented package requirement. IATA DGR (International Air Transport Association Dangerous Goods Regulations) and 49 CFR (the US DOT ground rules) both require documented training for anyone who classifies, packs, or signs — that's the certification you're booking. Add a temperature data logger in the box and you have evidence for every excursion argument with a courier.

Case — your worldA partner lab ships you ex-vivo tissue on dry ice via FedEx. The box lacks the UN1845 label and net-weight marking → rejected at the depot → 30 hours at ambient → tissue dead, study slipped two weeks. Cost of the missing label: a $6,000 shipment and a customer's timeline. Your shipping SOP (Stage 1 deliverable) exists to make that impossible.

L5FMEA: failure thinking before the failureRisk Management

The idea

FMEA (Failure Mode and Effects Analysis) is RCA's proactive twin — instead of asking "why did it fail?" after the fact (your Apollo strength), you ask "how could it fail?" before. It's the standard formal tool for exactly the supplier risk analysis your job description names.

The concepts

For each step of a process (or each supplier), list failure modes (ways it can go wrong), each mode's effect (what happens downstream) and cause. Score three things 1–10: Severity (S) — how bad the effect is; Occurrence (O) — how likely the cause; Detection (D) — how likely you'd catch it before it hurts (10 = you'd never catch it). The classic RPN (Risk Priority Number) = S×O×D ranks what to fix first; the newer AIAG-VDA method replaces RPN with an Action Priority table (high/medium/low) because multiplication treats a 10-severity/low-occurrence catastrophe the same as a trivial-but-common annoyance — severity should dominate. Two rules keep FMEAs honest: score causes for occurrence (not vague modes), and treat it as a living document reviewed when anything changes — a dead FMEA is worse than none because it manufactures false confidence.

Case — your worldRow for gelatin supply: failure mode "lot ships at wrong Bloom strength"; effect "model stiffness out of spec, batch scrapped" (S=8); cause "supplier has no in-process Bloom check" (O=6 for a small maker); detection "we only see the CoA, no incoming test" (D=7). AP: high → actions: add incoming Bloom verification on first article + require the in-process check in the quality agreement. Notice how the FMEA row generates your L1 acceptance criteria and your contract clauses — the tools chain together.
10-minute taskThree rows for your most critical supplier: one material failure, one logistics failure (cold chain), one business failure (they exit the market). Score S/O/D honestly. Sunday you'll present these to yourself.

L6Safety stock & service level: the math of "just in case"Supply Chain Planning

The idea

You know EOQ (how much to order). Safety stock answers the other question: how much extra to hold because demand and lead times wobble. It converts "I feel like we should keep more" into a number you can defend to finance.

The concepts

Service level: the probability you don't stock out in a replenishment cycle — a choice, not a law. 95% means you accept being short about 1 cycle in 20; pushing toward 99.9% gets exponentially expensive. z-score: the number of standard deviations matching that choice (95% → z≈1.65; 99% → z≈2.33 — same z-table as your Minitab work). The workhorse formula: SS = z × σ_d × √LT — safety stock equals your service-level z times demand variability (σ_d, the standard deviation of demand per period) times the square root of lead time in periods. Lead-time variability adds a second term; the square root is why long lead times are expensive even when average demand is known. Reorder point = average demand during lead time + SS: when inventory hits it, order. The perishables caveat that makes your world special: shelf life caps how much buffer you can hold — when SS math says "hold 10 weeks" and shelf life says "6 weeks max," the difference must come from somewhere else: shorter supplier lead time, capacity reservation, or a second source. That tension IS your job.

Case — your worldA culture medium: demand ~4 units/week, σ_d = 2, lead time 3 weeks, target 95%. SS = 1.65 × 2 × √3 ≈ 5.7 → hold 6 units beyond the 12 you expect to use during lead time; reorder at 18. Now check shelf life: at 4/week, 18 units on the shelf must survive ~4.5 weeks — fine if dated 90 days, a problem if 30. If 30: negotiate split deliveries against a blanket PO instead of holding stock.
10-minute taskSunday's applied slot: run this formula on one real item with rough numbers from memory. Wrong inputs are fine — the point is owning the machinery.

L7Total cost of ownership & landed cost: why unit price liesFinance & Cost Analytics

The idea

The quote is not the cost. TCO (Total Cost of Ownership) counts everything a purchase costs across its life; landed cost is its logistics slice — what it costs to get the thing to your dock. For cold-chain biologicals, the gap between price and cost is enormous, so unit-price comparisons actively mislead.

The concepts

TCO buckets: acquisition (price, ordering effort, qualification cost), ownership (freight, duty, storage, inspection, yield loss, expiry loss — for perishables often the biggest hidden line), post-ownership (disposal, failure costs downstream). Landed cost build-up: price + freight + insurance + customs duty + brokerage + special handling (validated cold-chain packaging can run $50–300 per shipment) + inbound QC. Incoterms (International Commercial Terms, ICC): three-letter codes that say where cost and risk transfer from seller to buyer — EXW (Ex Works: you own it at their door — cheap-looking quote, all risk yours), FOB (Free On Board — classic ocean term), DAP/DDP (Delivered At Place / Duty Paid: they carry it to you). Two quotes with different Incoterms are different products. The decision rule: compute cost per usable unit, delivered — (landed cost + expected expiry and rejection losses) ÷ units actually consumed.

Case — your worldSupplier A: $180/kg, MOQ 25 kg, EXW their dock, overnight cold ship $220. Supplier B: $210/kg, MOQ 5 kg, DAP. You use 2 kg/month, shelf life 12 months. A: you'll expire ~1 kg for every 24 used at best, pay all freight, and tie up cash. Per usable kg delivered, B likely wins despite a 17% higher sticker — and carries less write-off risk. This is the math that justifies your low-MOQ supplier hunt (your shortlist literally has a "Low-MOQ fit" column — you were already doing TCO by instinct).

L8Acceptance sampling: deciding on a lot without testing it allIndustrial Engineering

The idea

You can't inspect everything — testing may be destructive (very true for tissue) or just uneconomic. Acceptance sampling is the statistics of judging a whole lot from a sample, with known, chosen risks. It's the single most "new" high-value tool in your quality toolkit, and it's the heart of the CSQP/CQE exams.

The concepts

A lot arrives; you inspect a random sample of n; if defects ≤ acceptance number c, the lot passes. AQL (Acceptance Quality Limit): the worst defect rate you'd still call "acceptable on average" — a contract number, agreed with the supplier. Two mistakes are always possible: rejecting a good lot (producer's risk, α, usually ~5%) and accepting a bad one (consumer's risk, β, usually ~10%). The OC curve (Operating Characteristic) plots the probability of accepting a lot against its true defect rate — every sampling plan IS its OC curve, and reading one tells you exactly what protection you bought. ANSI/ASQ Z1.4: the standard lookup tables (lot size + inspection level → sample size code → n and c). c=0 plans: smaller samples but any single defect rejects — popular in med-device because "we accept some defects" reads badly. For your biologicals: destructive testing means sampling can't carry the load alone — so you shift assurance upstream: CoA-based acceptance (trust the supplier's measured certificate, verify their measurement system once) plus process audits, sampling only what's non-destructive (packaging, temperature logs, documentation, weights).

Case — your world500 fixation cassettes arrive. Z1.4, general inspection level II → code H → n=50, and at AQL 1.0% accept on ≤1 defect, reject on 2. You now know precisely what you're claiming — and reading the OC curve shows a lot that's truly 5% defective still passes ~28% of the time, which is why repeat offenders get tightened inspection, and why L1's SCAR loop exists.

L9P2P and the three-way match: the plumbing of buyingBusiness Analysis / ERP

The idea

You mapped P2P at Navy scale; now you live inside it as the buyer. The P2P (Procure-to-Pay) cycle is the same everywhere, and its control heart — the three-way match — is what auditors check first, especially when one person does everything.

The concepts

The chain: PR (Purchase Requisition) — the internal "we need this," with approval; PO (Purchase Order) — the external, legally binding order (in SAP, its T&Cs govern unless a contract exists); GR (Goods Receipt) — the recorded "it arrived, this much, this condition"; IR (Invoice Receipt) — the supplier's bill entered against the PO. The three-way match: before anyone pays, PO = GR = Invoice must agree in item, quantity, and price within tolerance; mismatches become exceptions a human resolves. Why it matters: it's the structural control against paying for what never arrived, duplicate invoices, and price creep. Blanket PO: one approved order releasing many deliveries — your tool for split deliveries (L7). Segregation of duties: ideally the person who orders ≠ receives ≠ pays; as a one-person function you ARE all three, so you design compensating controls: documented approvals above a threshold, someone else clicking GR, a monthly exception review someone else sees. Write that one page now, and a future customer audit finds a control environment instead of a shrug.

Case — your worldInvoice arrives for 10 kg; GR says 8 kg (2 kg short-shipped). Without a match: paid in full, loss invisible, supplier never corrects. With it: exception blocks payment, credit memo requested — and the shortage lands in supplier scorecard data (L1/L2 chaining again).

L10BATNA: negotiating when you're the small buyerBusiness Strategy

The idea

Negotiation power isn't volume or charm — it's your BATNA (Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement): what you'll actually do if this deal falls through. Whoever has the better walk-away wins the terms. Sole-source buying with no leverage is the hardest setting there is, which is why method matters more for you than for anyone at a big company.

The concepts

Reservation point: the worst deal you'd still accept — decided before the conversation, from your BATNA, never during it. ZOPA (Zone Of Possible Agreement): the overlap between your reservation point and theirs; if it's empty, no skill closes the deal. The prep insight: improve your BATNA before the table — qualifying a second source (even partially) moves your reservation point and changes the deal without a word spoken; that's why L2's bottleneck strategy and this lesson are the same project. Distributive vs. integrative: haggling over one number (price) splits a fixed pie; adding issues — volume commitment, capacity reservation, lead time, payment terms, lot dating, forecast sharing — lets you trade things you value cheaply for things they value dearly, growing the pie. Small buyers win integratively, almost never distributively. Anchoring: the first number placed shapes the whole range; when you have data (your L7 landed-cost model, a PPI index), anchor first with justification; when you don't, don't. And always: the relationship survives the deal — you'll need this supplier next quarter.

Case — your worldSole-source renewal, they open with +12% "inflation." Distributive response (argue to +8%) leaves you a nuisance account paying more. Integrative: "I can commit to 12 months of volume and share our quarterly forecast; in exchange, hold pricing at +4% (PPI for your inputs is +3.1%) and reserve two production slots per quarter." You traded certainty (cheap for you, valuable for a small maker) for price and capacity (what you actually need). Your Smithfield SLA instincts already work this way — now it has names.

L11Your first hour of SQL — you already think in itData Analytics & AI

The idea

SQL (Structured Query Language) is how you ask questions of databases directly — no export, no workbook limits. Here's the secret: you already know the concepts from Power Query; only the words change. This lesson is a translation table plus 30 minutes of typing.

The translation table

A table = a Power Query table (rows/columns). SELECT col1, col2 FROM table = "Choose Columns." WHERE condition = "Filter Rows." GROUP BY supplier + AVG(x) = "Group By" with an aggregation. JOIN = "Merge Queries" — LEFT JOIN is exactly Power Query's default left outer merge, matching rows from a second table by key and keeping all of the first. ORDER BY = sort. That's 80% of daily SQL. The genuinely new ideas come later (window functions, CTEs — Sprint 3+); today is proving to yourself the wall is paper.

Case — your worldThe supplier OTD query you'd want:
SELECT s.name, COUNT(*) AS receipts,
  AVG(CASE WHEN r.received_date <= r.promised_date THEN 1.0 ELSE 0 END) AS otd_rate
FROM receipts r JOIN suppliers s ON r.supplier_id = s.id
WHERE r.received_date >= '2026-01-01'
GROUP BY s.name ORDER BY otd_rate;
