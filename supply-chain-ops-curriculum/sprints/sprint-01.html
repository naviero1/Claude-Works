Module 1 guiding questions — answer out loud on Sun Sep 20

1. What makes a problem supervised learning rather than unsupervised? Give one example from your job of each. (Supervised = you have labeled right answers to learn from, e.g., past lots labeled accepted/rejected. Unsupervised = no labels, find structure, e.g., clustering suppliers by behavior.)

2. In your own words: what does the cost function J(w,b) measure, and why square the errors? (Average "wrongness" of the line across all training points; squaring makes negatives count, punishes big misses, and gives a smooth bowl gradient descent can roll down.)

3. What does the learning rate α control, and what goes wrong if it's too large? Too small? (Step size downhill; too large overshoots and diverges, too small crawls.)

4. Frame "predict supplier lead time" as a regression problem: what's the target, what 4 features would you use? (There's no single right answer — this is the question that turns the course into your job.)

Module 2 guiding questions — answer out loud on Sun Sep 27

1. Why does feature scaling make gradient descent converge faster? (Unscaled features make the cost bowl a long narrow valley; scaling rounds it so steps head straight downhill.)

2. What is vectorization, and why is np.dot(w,x) faster than a Python loop? (One optimized parallel operation on whole arrays instead of interpreted one-at-a-time steps.)

3. When would polynomial features beat a straight line? Give an operations example. (Curved reality — e.g., overtime hours vs. throughput flattens; changeover cost vs. batch size bends.)

4. Your LMS-Light bands predicted units-picked-per-hour from operational data. Which engineered features would a regression version have used — and what would you add now that you couldn't then?

L1The supplier quality loop: NCR → 8D → SCAR → CAPASupplier Engineering
  • NCR (Nonconformance Report): if it isn't written down, it never happened.
  • Containment before investigation — quarantine first, think second.
  • The diamond is judgment: not every hiccup earns a SCAR (Supplier Corrective Action Request); minor ones become scorecard trend data.
  • 8D is a standard envelope around root-cause work you already master (it's D4 of eight); CAPA isn't closed until effectiveness is verified later — the dashed return arrow is the most-skipped step in industry.
Case — your worldA gelatin lot's CoA shows 210 Bloom against a 250 spec. NCR → quarantine → return. Second occurrence in 3 months → SCAR; the supplier's 8D finds no in-process Bloom check; they add one. Your next 5 CoAs confirm it held — loop closed, and the whole story is three rows in your NCR log.
10-minute taskDesign your NCR log columns (date, item, lot, supplier, problem, containment, disposition, SCAR? Y/N, status, closed date). That sheet is the seed of your company's quality system.
Depth — the full write-up

NCR: the written record that something didn't meet spec — what, which lot, how found. Containment: immediately fence off suspect material (quarantine shelf, hold label) so nobody uses it while you investigate. Disposition: the decision — use-as-is (deviation approved), rework, return, or scrap. 8D (Eight Disciplines): D1 team, D2 problem description, D3 containment, D4 root cause, D5 chosen corrective action, D6 implement, D7 prevent recurrence system-wide, D8 recognize the team. SCAR: the formal letter asking a supplier to run an 8D and report back — for serious or repeat problems, not every hiccup. CAPA (Corrective and Preventive Action): the umbrella system tracking fixes to closure, including verification of effectiveness weeks later. COPQ (Cost of Poor Quality): what failures cost in dollars — your prioritization language.

ON YOUR DRIVE: ASQ CQE Handbook 5th ed. (corrective action chapters) · The Quality Toolbox (Tague). BUY: Root Cause Analysis — Duke Okes (Amazon)

L2The Kraljic matrix: your supply base on one 2×2Procurement
  • Axes are impact (spend + consequence of failure) × supply risk (few suppliers, switching cost, scarcity).
  • Bottleneck is the trap quadrant: cheap items that stop the line. Strategy is supply security, never price pressure.
  • Strategic items get relationships and joint planning; routine items get automated; leverage items get competition.
  • Items move — the dashed arrow is your gelatin as production scales.
Case — your worldYour low-MOQ supplier hunt is textbook bottleneck strategy — you were doing it by instinct. The Sigma/Thermo ASL items deserve zero relationship energy; the tissue network deserves most of it.
10-minute taskPlace your top 10 purchased items on the 2×2. Mark each with today's actual behavior; circle every mismatch.
Depth — the full write-up

Kraljic (HBR, 1983) remains the anchor framework of category management. Routine: minimize effort; catalogs, blanket POs. Leverage: buyer holds power; competitive bidding, easy switching. Bottleneck: buffer stock, alternate qualification, monitoring — accept paying a little more for certainty. Strategic: long-term agreements, top-to-top relationships, joint capacity planning; treat the supplier as an extension of the company. The matrix also drives risk segmentation (Sprint 1's FMEA targets = the right column) and negotiation posture (L10).

ON YOUR DRIVE: Leading Procurement Strategy 3rd ed. (category strategy chapters). BUY: Category Management in Purchasing — O'Brien (Amazon) · Kraljic's original HBR article (1983).

L3Supplier market power — and how they see YOUR accountEconomics
  • Structure spectrum: more supplier power to the right → price pressure works less, relationship and alternatives work more.
  • Switching costs give even a non-monopoly supplier power over you (the hold-up problem).
  • A small ex-vivo buyer starts in the bottom half of every supplier's book; the escape is being cheap to serve and interesting to keep.
Case — your worldYour tissue source is near-monopoly: haggling is pointless and can hurt. A 6-month forecast, on-time payment, and easy logistics move you from "nuisance" to "development" — worth more than any discount when supply gets tight.
10-minute taskTop 3 suppliers, two words each: the structure you face; the box you occupy in their ranking. Any "monopoly + nuisance" pair is your most urgent relationship project.
Depth — the full write-up

The spectrum runs perfect competition (many sellers, identical product, nobody sets prices) → monopolistic competition (many sellers, differentiated) → oligopoly (few sellers who watch each other) → monopoly (one seller sets terms). Monopsony is one powerful buyer — you are the opposite. Switching costs: requalification, revalidation, and risk of changing suppliers; high switching costs transfer power to the incumbent. Supplier preferencing: suppliers segment accounts as core / development / exploitable / nuisance based on attractiveness and value; small buyers win by account behavior, not volume.

ON YOUR DRIVE: Mankiw, Principles of Microeconomics (ch. 15–17). BUY: Understanding Michael Porter — Magretta (Amazon)

L4Shipping biological materials legally: UN3373, dry ice, triple packagingWarehouse & Logistics
  • Three outcomes, one decision tree — and the tree must be walked by a trained person (IATA DGR / 49 CFR requirement).
  • Most research material lands in Category B (UN3373, P650) or exempt; Category A is rare and changes everything.
  • Dry ice is a dangerous good even when the specimen is exempt.
  • A data logger in the box turns every excursion dispute into evidence.
Case — your worldA partner ships tissue on dry ice; the box lacks the UN1845 label and net weight → FedEx rejects at the depot → 30 hours at ambient → dead tissue, slipped study. Cost of a missing $0.10 label: a $6,000 shipment.
10-minute taskClassify three shipments in writing: (a) formalin-fixed tissue blocks, (b) fresh porcine organs on dry ice, (c) a suspect infectious human sample. Category, packing instruction, markings. ((a) likely exempt; (b) UN3373 + UN1845; (c) Category A — refuse until trained.)
Depth — the full write-up

Category A (UN2814 human / UN2900 animal): infectious substances capable of permanent disability or fatal disease — P620, restricted. Category B (UN3373): infectious but below that threshold — P650. Exempt: documented judgment by a trained person. Triple packaging: leakproof primary + absorbent, leakproof secondary, rigid outer. Dry ice UN1845: Class 9, vented package, net-weight marking. IATA DGR and 49 CFR both require documented shipper training with 24-month recurrency — the compliance-floor course.

Free: IATA "Infectious Substances Shipping Guidelines" summary · CDC shipping guidance. Course: Saf-T-Pak IATA training. ON YOUR DRIVE: Industrial Refrigeration Handbook (Stoecker) for cold-side physics.

  • The output isn't the score — it's the action list, which feeds your acceptance criteria (L8), contracts (L10), and buffers (L6).

Case — your worldThe three rows above are your starter supplier FMEA — notice each action points at another lesson in this sprint. That chaining is the whole design.
10-minute taskRewrite the three rows for your actual most-critical supplier: one material failure, one logistics failure, one business failure. Honest scores.
Depth — the full write-up

FMEA (Failure Mode and Effects Analysis) is the proactive twin of RCA. Classic RPN = S×O×D ranks priorities; the AIAG-VDA handbook replaced it with an Action Priority table because multiplication under-weights severity. Variants: design FMEA, process FMEA, and the supplier FMEA you'll run (failure modes across quote-to-delivery). Rules of craft: occurrence belongs to causes, not vague modes; detection scores your current controls; the document lives — review triggers include new lots, new processes, any SCAR.

ON YOUR DRIVE: Supply Chain Risk Management (Schlegel & Trent) · CQE Handbook (FMEA section). BUY: The Basics of FMEA — McDermott et al. (Amazon)

L6Safety stock & service level: the math of "just in case"Supply Chain Planning
  • Service level is a choice (95% ≈ short 1 cycle in 20); its z-score prices the buffer: SS = z·σ_d·√LT.
  • ROP = demand during lead time + SS — order when stock hits it, not when it feels low.
  • The √LT term is why long lead times are expensive even when demand is steady.
  • Perishables twist: shelf life caps the buffer; when the math wants more than dating allows, the fix moves upstream — shorter lead time, split deliveries, capacity reservation, second source.
Case — your worldThe chart's numbers are the medium case: SS 6, ROP 18. Check dating: 18 units at 4/week must survive ~4.5 weeks — fine at 90-day dating, broken at 30. At 30 days, negotiate split deliveries on a blanket PO instead of holding stock (L9's tool).
10-minute taskSunday's applied slot: run the formula on one real item with rough numbers from memory. Wrong inputs are fine — own the machinery.
Depth — the full write-up

Service level: probability of not stocking out in a replenishment cycle; z-scores: 90%→1.28, 95%→1.65, 98%→2.05, 99%→2.33 — cost grows exponentially as you chase nines. Full formula with lead-time variability: SS = z·√(LT·σ_d² + d²·σ_LT²). Reorder point systems (continuous review, s,Q) vs. periodic review (R,S) — you'll mostly run periodic at a small company. Perishable inventory: expiry converts carrying cost into outright loss; expiry-aware policies cap order-up-to levels at shelf-life-consumable quantities.

ON YOUR DRIVE: Supply Chain Logistics Management (Bowersox — inventory chapters). Free: MITx SC1x (edX). BUY: Supply Chain Management — Chopra & Meindl (Amazon)

L7Total cost of ownership & landed cost: why unit price liesFinance & Cost Analytics
  • TCO = acquisition + ownership + post-ownership; for perishables the killer hidden line is expiry loss.
  • Landed cost stacks freight, insurance, duty, brokerage, special handling, inbound QC on top of price.
  • Incoterms decide who pays and who carries risk — an EXW quote and a DAP quote are different products.
  • Decision rule: (landed cost + expected losses) ÷ units actually consumed.
Case — your worldYour shortlist's "Low-MOQ fit" column was TCO instinct before you had the name — the chart is that instinct with arithmetic attached, ready for a CFO.
10-minute taskSunday: a 10-row landed-cost sheet for one real item — price, freight, packaging, duty, inspection time at your loaded rate, expected expiry loss. Compare per usable unit.
Depth — the full write-up

TCO buckets: acquisition (price, ordering effort, qualification cost), ownership (freight, duty, storage, inspection, yield loss, expiry), post-ownership (disposal, downstream failure costs). Incoterms 2020 highlights: EXW (you own it at their door), FOB (loaded on vessel), DAP/DDP (delivered, duty unpaid/paid). Validated cold-chain packaging runs $50–300 per shipment and belongs in every biologics landed cost. Assumptions behind the chart: A's 25 kg at 2 kg/month with 9 months of dating on arrival → ~18 kg usable; $4,500 product + $220 freight over 18 usable kg ≈ $262.

ON YOUR DRIVE: Lysons & Farrington · Leading Procurement Strategy. BUY: Monczka et al. (Amazon)

L8Acceptance sampling: deciding on a lot without testing it allIndustrial Engineering
  • Inspect n=50 at random; accept if defects ≤ c=1 — the whole plan.
  • AQL is a contract number: "this defect rate should almost always pass." α = good lots wrongly rejected; β = bad lots wrongly passed.
  • The middle dot is the humbling one: sampling is a screen, not a wall — which is why it pairs with supplier process control, not replaces it.
  • Destructive tissue testing → shift assurance upstream: CoA-based acceptance + supplier audits; sample the non-destructive (docs, temps, weights, packaging).
Case — your world500 fixation cassettes arrive: Z1.4 level II → code H → n=50, Ac=1/Re=2 at AQL 1.0. You can now state exactly what "accepted" means — and defend it to a customer auditor.
10-minute taskWithout looking, sketch this curve's shape freehand and mark AQL and β. If you can draw it, you own it.
Depth — the full write-up

ANSI/ASQ Z1.4: lot size + inspection level → sample-size code letter → n and c; includes normal/tightened/reduced switching rules that operationalize supplier trust. c=0 plans (Squeglia): smaller n, any defect rejects — popular in med-device optics. Variables plans (Z1.9) use measurements instead of pass/fail for smaller samples. For research-grade biologicals: CoA-based acceptance (verify the supplier's measurement system once — MSA — then trust-but-trend their numbers), skip-lot for proven suppliers, 100% inspection only where non-destructive and cheap.

ON YOUR DRIVE: Acceptance Sampling in Quality Control (Schilling & Neubauer) — the definitive text · CQE Handbook · SPC for Real-World Applications (Levinson). BUY: Montgomery (Amazon)

L9P2P and the three-way match: the plumbing of buyingBusiness Analysis / ERP
  • PR → PO → GR → IR → match → pay: the same cycle at your company and at NAVSUP scale.
  • The match blocks: wrong quantity, wrong price, duplicate invoices — and generates the exception data your scorecard (L1) eats.
  • Blanket PO: one approval, many releases — the split-delivery tool L6 and L7 both reached for.
  • Segregation of duties: order ≠ receive ≠ pay. As a one-person function, write your compensating controls down before a customer auditor asks.
Case — your worldInvoice 10 kg, GR 8 kg → without a match: paid in full, loss invisible. With it: payment blocked, credit memo, and the short-ship lands in the supplier's OTD data.
10-minute taskBPMN-sketch your actual P2P in 8–12 boxes, mark who does each step, circle every step where you're both actor and checker, and write one compensating control per circle. (Stage 2 deliverable seed.)
Depth — the full write-up

PR (Purchase Requisition): internal request with approval. PO (Purchase Order): the binding external order; its T&Cs govern absent a contract. GR (Goods Receipt): recorded arrival — quantity and condition. IR (Invoice Receipt): the bill entered against the PO. Three-way match tolerance policy (e.g., ±2% price, ±5% qty) is a design decision. Compensating controls for a sole buyer: documented approvals above a threshold, a different person clicking GR, monthly exception review someone else signs, and an audit trail (your ERP does this if you let it).

ON YOUR DRIVE: Baily et al. 12th ed. · Lysons & Farrington. Free: SAP Learning Journey "Sourcing and Procurement in S/4HANA" (learning.sap.com).

← Capacity reservation, price hold, lot dating, priority allocation…what you ask for in exchange
  • BATNA sets your reservation point; improving it (partially qualifying an alternate) changes the deal without a word spoken — L2's bottleneck strategy and this lesson are the same project.
  • Empty ZOPA = no deal any skill can close; find out early.
  • Small buyers win integratively (multi-issue trades), almost never distributively (price haggling).
  • Anchor first only when you have data (your L7 model, a PPI index); otherwise don't.
Case — your worldThe figure IS the case: their +12% opener, your data-anchored +4%, settlement at +4.5% with the capacity reservation you actually needed. Certainty is cheap for you and precious to a small maker — that asymmetry is your whole negotiating endowment.
10-minute taskSunday: the one-page plan for your next real renewal — honest BATNA, reservation point, target, three trades from the table, your anchor and its data.
Depth — the full write-up

BATNA (Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement): what you'll actually do if talks fail. Reservation point: worst acceptable deal, derived from BATNA before the table. ZOPA (Zone Of Possible Agreement): overlap of the two reservation points. Distributive vs. integrative: claiming a fixed pie vs. growing it with multi-issue trades. Anchoring: first credible number shapes the range. Relationship rule: you need this supplier next quarter — the deal must leave them wanting your account.
