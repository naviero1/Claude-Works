# Peripheral Topics — Multi-Criteria Only

These sit outside or at the edge of the 11 core domains. Per your rule, each is listed **only because it pays under at least two criteria**, and each ends with **one refining question** — answer it before investing, and skip any topic whose answer comes back weak. Criteria vocabulary: *current-role value · market/transfer value · builds on an existing strength · credential leverage · future-proofing · network/community value.*

---

## 1. FDA preclinical & medical-device testing context (your customers' regulatory world)
**What:** How customers actually use ex-vivo models — 510(k)/PMA bench-testing evidence, IDE, ISO 10993 biocompatibility, design controls (21 CFR 820.30), and what a GLP study demands of its material inputs. Literacy in the downstream ecosystem, not quality-system implementation (that's core).
**Criteria:** current-role · market value in biotech/med-device · future-proofing · credential leverage.
**Why for you:** Understanding what a customer's ISO 10993 or bench-test protocol requires of tissue provenance lets you set supplier acceptance criteria that anticipate customer audits — turning you from internal buyer into commercial asset. Your NAVSUP compliance-documentation muscle makes this cheap.
**Entry point:** FDA CDRH Learn free video modules on 510(k)/PMA (one weekend), then skim ISO 10993-1's framework table.
**Refining question:** *Do your customers ever ask for documentation about tissue provenance, handling, or model characterization to support their regulatory submissions — and who answers those questions today?*

## 2. Lab-science literacy for the non-scientist
**What:** Enough working biology to hold your own with R&D and biological suppliers — what keeps tissue viable, cell culture and media basics, aseptic technique, contamination modes, how scientists define "quality" for living material. Conversational and specification fluency, not bench skills.
**Criteria:** current-role · builds on strength (spec-writing, acceptance criteria) · future-proofing in the biotech-ops niche.
**Why for you:** You write acceptance criteria and negotiate specs with scientists daily; without shared vocabulary you take their requirements on faith — exactly the dependency a 20-year IE who builds his own labor standards has never tolerated elsewhere. Your record of rapidly acquiring domain language (meat yields, WMS data models, Navy logistics) says ~20 hours, not a degree.
**Entry point:** ATCC's free Animal Cell Culture Guide (~40 pages) + a 30-minute tour from one staff scientist on what kills their tissue models.
**Refining question:** *In supplier or R&D conversations this month, how many times did a biological term come up that you couldn't independently evaluate — and did it change a buying decision?*

## 3. The 3Rs movement & the market for animal-testing alternatives (NAMs)
**What:** Replacement-Reduction-Refinement, IACUC oversight, and the policy landscape (FDA Modernization Act 2.0; FDA's 2025 roadmap to reduce animal testing) actively creating the market your employer sells into. Strategic market context, not ethics compliance.
**Criteria:** current-role (mission/customer fluency) · future-proofing (policy-driven growth market) · network value (distinctive sector conversation).
**Why for you:** A supplier engineer who can articulate *why demand for the company's models will grow* contributes to capacity and sourcing strategy above his title — and NAMs is a young field with no established supply-chain talent pool. You'd be early.
**Entry point:** FDA's April 2025 "Roadmap to Reducing Animal Testing in Preclinical Safety Studies" (~20 pages, public) + one NIH NICEATM overview page.
**Refining question:** *Does leadership talk about the FDA's animal-testing-reduction push as a growth driver — and would a one-page brief from you on its supply-side implications land, or be seen as out of lane?*

## 4. Technical writing & documentation craft
**What:** Writing documents people can act on — plain-language principles, document typing (procedure vs. reference vs. tutorial, Diátaxis-style), editing technique. Distinct from the core BRD/spec content, which covers *what* to write, not *how* to write well.
**Criteria:** current-role · builds on strength (BRDs, BPMN docs, trainings) · market value (portable everywhere).
**Why for you:** Without ISO 13485, every quality agreement, SOP, protocol, and spec is authored from scratch — mostly by you. Nearly every deliverable in all five stages is ultimately a document; craft multiplies the whole plan. As a non-native English speaker competing at principal level, deliberate writing craft is quiet insurance.
**Entry point:** Google's free Technical Writing One course (half a day), then rewrite one existing SOP with it. Or Zinsser's *On Writing Well*.
**Refining question:** *When your documents fail at work, do they fail on structure and clarity, or on missing content and authority — which is the actual bottleneck?*

## 5. Instructional design & training as a deliberate career asset
**What:** Adult-learning principles, training design (objectives, practice design, Kirkpatrick evaluation), packaging know-how into courses. You already build trainings instinctively; this makes it a namable, sellable capability.
**Criteria:** builds on strength (15-year training record: 5S/SMED/TPM/RCA rollouts, prompt engineering at Vintun) · market value (training is a monetizable consulting product) · current-role (every new QMS process needs trained users).
**Why for you:** A no-degree expert's most credible consulting wedge is teaching what he has demonstrably done. This converts a personality trait into a product.
**Entry point:** *Design for How People Learn* (Julie Dirksen); retrofit one training you're proud of against its checklist.
**Refining question:** *If you packaged one training you've already built into a polished 2-hour course, who would pay for it or promote you for it — your employer, a professional chapter, or a paying client?*

## 6. Sustainability & Scope 3 supply chain (ESG for buyers)
**What:** How environmental/social requirements land on procurement — Scope 3 accounting basics, supplier sustainability questionnaires (EcoVadis/CDP-style), sustainable packaging and cold-chain trade-offs, ESG clauses.
**Criteria:** market value (screening keyword in senior sourcing roles; CPSM now examines it) · future-proofing · current-role (cold-chain packaging waste and single-use plastics are live lab-supply issues).
**Why for you:** Cheap for you specifically — Scope 3 is data plumbing (spend cube × emission factors), a direct reuse of your Power BI/ETL strength. Modest current urgency; a next-tier peripheral, but skipping it entirely would date your profile within five years.
**Entry point:** free GHG Protocol Scope 3 e-learning module, then a rough carbon estimate of your top-five spend categories from your own spend cube.
**Refining question:** *Have customers or investors asked for sustainability information yet — and will your CPSM/CIPS study already cover the ESG content, making separate study redundant?*

## 7. Systems thinking & system dynamics
**What:** Feedback loops, stocks and flows, delays, unintended consequences — the Meadows/Senge/Sterman school. The bullwhip effect in the core maps is one instance; this is the general discipline behind it.
**Criteria:** builds on strength (20 years of cross-functional loops by instinct) · current-role (the "all over the place" role is a system-design problem) · market value (lingua franca of senior strategy conversations).
**Why for you:** The formal language would let you *diagram* why your procurement-capacity-risk duties interlock and communicate that upward — precisely your stated pain. It compounds three core domains at once (Risk, Strategy, SCM) rather than adding a fourth silo: the definition of a high-leverage peripheral.
**Entry point:** *Thinking in Systems* (Donella Meadows, ~200 pages), then one causal-loop diagram of your own supplier-readiness system.
**Refining question:** *When you explain your role's interlocking duties to leadership, do they get it — or would a one-page systems diagram change a real upcoming conversation (headcount, scope, budget)?*

## 8. Knowledge management & SOP systems for a small company
**What:** SOP hierarchies and lightweight document control, searchable knowledge bases, capturing tribal knowledge, plus a personal knowledge system for a heavy self-directed learner.
**Criteria:** current-role (a no-13485 company has no forced documentation discipline — someone must invent it) · builds on strength (BPMN capture, Confluence, training creation) · future-proofing (this 11-domain curriculum needs a retrieval system to compound).
**Why for you:** The plan prescribes dozens of artifacts and no system for finding them; and you personally are about to ingest an enormous curriculum — without deliberate note/retrieval practice much of the investment evaporates. A multiplier on every other topic.
**Entry point:** one shared knowledge base (Notion/Confluence) with a three-level SOP/reference structure; migrate your five most-used supplier documents into it.
**Refining question:** *If you left the company for three weeks tomorrow, what supplier knowledge exists only in your head — and is fixing that officially anyone's job?*

## 9. Professional community involvement: ASQ / ISM / ASCM / IISE chapters
**What:** Attend → present → hold a small office in local chapters of the societies whose exams you're already taking. The Research Triangle's chapters are dense with biotech and manufacturing employers.
**Criteria:** network value · credential leverage (recert units, study groups) · market value (warm referrals bypass degree-based resume screens).
**Why for you:** For a no-degree candidate, peer standing and referrals are the single most effective bypass of HR degree filters — and your training-delivery skill means you can be a *presenter* within months, which also generates the public artifacts the strategy calls for.
**Entry point:** one local ASQ section meeting (Raleigh) this month; introduce yourself to the program chair — chapters are chronically short of speakers with real stories.
**Refining question:** *Given limited evening hours, which single society's chapter aligns with your first certification target (ASQ for CSQP vs. ISM for CPSM) — and would you commit to presenting your vendor-accountability toolkit story there within six months?*

## 10. Bilingual leverage: professional Spanish & Latin-America supplier development
**What:** Upgrading native Spanish into a commercial instrument — technical/contractual Spanish for procurement and quality, LatAm business culture and supplier development, positioning as the person who can open, audit, and manage Spanish-speaking suppliers.
**Criteria:** builds on strength (native bilingual, Peru industry experience) · market value (nearshoring demand for bilingual US-based supplier engineers) · future-proofing.
**Why for you:** "Can conduct a supplier audit in Spanish in Monterrey" is a rare, concrete differentiator as USMCA-driven nearshoring accelerates. The gap is small: terminology and market knowledge, not language. Honest caveat: your current biological supply base may be entirely domestic — this may be a pure market/career play, which is what the question must establish.
**Entry point:** a two-page bilingual glossary of your 100 most-used procurement/quality terms; add "Spanish-language supplier audits and negotiations" to LinkedIn.
**Refining question:** *Three years out, do you want your next role to have an international/LatAm dimension at all — or is your target market (biotech supplier quality) so domestic that this stays a dormant asset?*

## 11. Public technical portfolio: open-source tools & written case studies
**What:** Publishing sanitized, working versions of the tools this curriculum produces — a supplier scorecard template, a safety-stock calculator for intermittent demand, a Monte Carlo supply-risk notebook — on GitHub with short write-ups.
**Criteria:** credential leverage (verifiable proof no degree screen can dispute) · builds on strength (LMS-Light, RPM, the vendor toolkit — portfolio-grade tools locked inside past employers) · market value.
**Why for you:** Everything impressive you've built is proprietary and invisible. Public, runnable tools are the artifact engine behind the positioning narrative, they double as your learning method (you learn by building), and they feed the consulting ambition with demo-able assets. Marginal cost is low — each stage's deliverables naturally throw one off; the discipline is sanitizing and finishing.
**Entry point:** one GitHub repo with one finished artifact: a generic supplier scorecard template + a 500-word README on the statistical-banding method behind it.
**Refining question:** *Are you willing to spend the ~20% extra "finishing" effort to make one tool per quarter public and generic — and does your employment agreement permit publishing generalized versions of methods you develop?*
