# Decision memo: Should we add a second supplier for the PVA solution now?

**Prepared for:** Division leadership
**Prepared by:** Sourcing engineering
**Basis:** Six fact-checked research packages (procurement frameworks, dual-vs-single sourcing literature, disruption-cost evidence, incumbent-strengthening techniques, warm-backup practice, regulatory). Only claims that the fact-checkers rated CONFIRMED or PLAUSIBLE are used; items they could not verify are marked *(unverified)*; refuted items were dropped.

**Bottom line.** The volume does not yet justify an active second supplier at a one-third share and $3.00/lb. That option costs ~$50,700 per year (it doubles the category spend), ~$310,000 over five years, and still leaves two-thirds of supply exposed unless PCI can ramp to 100% inside our 2.5-week time-to-survive. The defensible position is: stay single-source with SNP and strengthen that relationship with contractual, inventory and documentation controls now (Option B); finish PCI qualification and hold it as a warm, exercised backup at roughly $12,000 per year (Option C); and convert to active dual sourcing (Option A) only when defined triggers fire. The one number leadership must supply to make this rigorous is the weekly contribution margin of the device SKUs that depend on the hydrogel.

## 1. Framing: what kind of item this is, and why the numbers point the way they do

**The item.** A custom aqueous polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) solution, ~11% solids, cooked at 90-95 C, bought in 55-gal drums (450 lb), 18-day shelf life. Demand ~1,300 lb/week = 67,600 lb/yr = ~150 drums/yr, growing ~10%/yr. Annual spend $50,700 at SNP's $0.75/lb. Of ~94 US suppliers screened since March 2026, nearly all were eliminated (cannot hold the dissolution cook, or batch minimums exceed what the shelf life lets us use). One toll blender (CJB) quoted $8-8.50/lb toll excluding materials, ~10-11x SNP's all-in price. PCI is in lab-batch testing with promising results after ~6 months and is not yet qualified.

**Kraljic classification: a bottleneck item.** In Kraljic's 1983 purchasing portfolio (Harvard Business Review), items with low profit impact and high supply risk are "bottleneck items." His supply-risk criteria are "availability, number of suppliers, competitive demand, make-or-buy opportunities, and storage risks and substitution possibilities"; the 18-day shelf life is a storage risk in his terms. His prescription for this quadrant, verbatim: **"Volume insurance (at cost premium if necessary). Control of vendors. Security of inventories. Backup plans."** Required information: "medium-term supply/demand forecasts, very good market data, inventory costs, maintenance plans." Decision level: "higher level (e.g., department heads)." Two points follow. First, Kraljic expects this decided by leadership, not by a buyer, which is why this memo exists. Second, "security of inventories" is physically unavailable to us at the finished-solution stage (18 days), so the decision reduces to how much to pay for "backup plans" versus "control of vendors." (Kraljic's separate advice to "consolidate ... in a single supplier, accept high prices" is written for strategic items, not bottleneck items; it is an analogy for a weak-buyer position, not his rule for this quadrant.)

Gelderman & van Weele (2003), the standard empirical refinement of Kraljic, give bottleneck items exactly two directions: (1) "reduce dependence and risk, find other solutions" (a new supplier, or broadening the specification), or (2) "accept the dependence, reduce the negative consequences," whose common responses are "contingency planning in combination with risk analysis, consignment systems, long-term contracting with an emphasis on quality and assurance of supply, and ultimately keeping (extra) stocks." Option B is the recognised "hold" strategy; Options A and C are the "move" strategy. Their case evidence is that practitioners "move" only when it is "interesting enough, especially from an economic point of view."

**The exposure: time-to-survive (TTS) versus time-to-recover (TTR).** Simchi-Levi, Schmidt & Wei (HBR 2014; Interfaces 2015) define TTS as "the maximum amount of time the system can function without performance loss if a particular node is disrupted" and TTR as the time for a node to recover to full functionality. If TTS < TTR the firm is exposed for the difference.

| Metric | Value | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| TTS | ~2.5 weeks (less the age of drums already on hand) | 18-day shelf life; no inventory buffer possible |
| TTR, cold start of a new cooker | 26-52 weeks (6-12 months) | Our own experience: 94 screened, ~6 months in with PCI, not yet qualified; external norms 3-18 months for medical-device supplier qualification (VentureOutsource 2026: 3-6 months simple components, 6-12 months electronics, >18 months where biocompatibility data are needed); a CDMO cut customer qualification from 12 to 4 months only after obtaining ISO 13485 (MasterControl) |
| Uncovered window | 23-50 weeks of stopped hydrogel production | TTR minus TTS |

In business-continuity terms (ISO 22301), our maximum tolerable period of disruption is ~2.5 weeks and our current recovery time objective for a supplier loss is 6-12 months; the standard requires that gap to be closed by a chosen strategy or formally accepted by top management.

**Why the volume/spend level matters to the answer.** Four reasons, each evidenced:

1. *Spend is the wrong denominator for risk, but the right one for cost.* At Ford, across 4,534 supplier sites, the lost profit from a site disruption "is not correlated with the amount Ford spends at the supplier"; 408 sites were "very high" impact; hundreds had TTS under one week; "some of the largest exposures reside in unlikely places, such as the production and (or) procurement of low-cost, commoditized parts" (Simchi-Levi et al. 2015). This legitimises leadership attention on a $50k item. But it also means the premium must be judged against loss avoided, not against spend.
2. *Low spend removes leverage over both suppliers.* The same paper calls low-spend/high-impact suppliers "the most challenging": "Low total spend implies that the firm is not in a good position to require the supplier to have multiple sites ... engaging in a dual-sourcing strategy is difficult." Its remedy is to shift more volume and spend to the supplier to earn the right to demand resilience commitments, the opposite of splitting.
3. *Splitting makes us smaller to the supplier we depend on most.* Supplier preferencing (Steele & Court 1996) plots how a supplier values an account; practitioners use 1-3% of supplier turnover as the high/low boundary and "<1% of the supplier's divisional or company turnover" as the first marker of "nuisance" treatment. Our account is $50,700 / $4,000,000 = **1.27%** of SNP's revenue today. Diverting one-third to PCI drops it to $33,800 = **0.84%**, below the nuisance line, exactly when SNP's goodwill matters most. Kept whole at 10% growth it reaches 1.39% (yr 2), 1.53%, 1.69%, **1.86%** (yr 5), assuming SNP's revenue stays flat at $4M.
4. *Small volumes split between two makers raise unit cost on both sides and create no competition.* Silbermayr & Minner (2016): the benefit of dual sourcing "decreases with reliability" of the incumbent and with demand level; splitting forfeits economies of scale and learning. Defense procurement evidence (OSD 1987 draft; Lyon 2006): savings from dual sourcing versus sole sourcing "were not (except for a single case) found," benefits were "nonmonetary" and had to be weighed against "uniformly higher costs"; dual sourcing lowered cost only where it created later competition or followed incumbent quality problems. At 150 drums/yr there is no competitive upside; PCI is insurance and must be priced as insurance.

## 2. Option A: dual-source now (one-third to PCI at $3.00/lb)

### 2.1 Full cost picture

| Line | Amount | Note |
|---|---|---|
| PCI volume | 22,533 lb/yr (~50 drums) | one-third of 67,600 lb |
| PCI spend | $67,600/yr | at $3.00/lb |
| SNP spend | $33,800/yr | 45,067 lb at $0.75/lb |
| Total category spend | **$101,400/yr** | vs $50,700 today: spend doubles |
| Annual redundancy premium | **$50,700/yr** | 22,533 lb x ($3.00 - $0.75) |
| Premium, year 5 | $74,230 | 10%/yr volume growth |
| Five-year cumulative premium | **$309,529** | sum of yr 1-5 at 10% growth |
| Sensitivity to PCI price (one-third share) | $0.85/lb: $2,300; **$1.40/lb: $14,650**; $2.55/lb: $40,560; $3.00/lb: $50,700 | market triangulation low / base / high vs working assumption |

The $3.00/lb assumption is above the market triangulation's high case ($2.55). The premium is a 300% uplift; the range practitioners quote for a normal second source is 10-20% (LightSource 2026, a vendor blog; Kloepfel's own example is 15% at a 75/25 split). A 300% premium signals PCI is not at process or scale parity, which supports negotiating toward the $1.40 base as PCI matures.

### 2.2 What it actually buys

- **Redundancy for one-third of volume, immediately.** Two-thirds of supply remains exposed unless PCI can surge. If PCI's ramp from one-third to full rate takes 4-8 weeks, an SNP failure still stops production for roughly 2-6 weeks (ramp minus 2.5-week TTS). PCI's kettle capacity at 90-95 C, batch size versus 450-lb drums, maximum weekly output, ramp time and price at 100% volume are unknown today and must be confirmed in writing before crediting Option A with full loss avoidance.
- **A continuously exercised second source**, which generates the delivery, incoming-inspection and certificate-of-analysis (CoA) record that supplier re-evaluation under ISO 13485 clause 7.4.1 requires, and which cannot go stale.
- **Learning at PCI on our recipe**, which shortens future TTR if SNP exits.

Operations-research results on split ratios: a 50:50 split is never optimal even for identical suppliers; a 75:25 allocation is "a very robust policy" (Silbermayr & Minner 2016). If we ever go dual, 67:33 to 80:20 is the right range; it is the standing premium, not the ratio, that is the problem here.

### 2.3 Hidden costs

- **Qualification (one-time, incurred in A and C alike).** FDA's 510(k) change guidance (Oct 2017, Section C) treats "any change in supplier" of a material as a "yes" at question C2 and expects analysis "even when these changes result in materials that remain within the original material specifications," plus a biocompatibility risk assessment. Practically: production-scale PCI lot, hydrogel verification lots, documented change assessment (letter-to-file at minimum), ISO 10993 re-evaluation (targeted panels ~$4,000 and 4 weeks at one lab; full panels far more *(NAMSA's $150-500k figure unverified)*), process validation. Internal estimate $15-40k; the team's logged hours on PCI to date are the best local number.
- **Two sets of quality control (QC).** Incoming inspection on two suppliers' lots (viscosity, % solids, pH, appearance, bioburden as applicable), two CoA trend streams, two change-control streams, two supplier audits and re-evaluations, two quality agreements. An electronics example puts ongoing dual-supplier overhead at $12,000/yr (SupplyICs 2026, illustrative); a simpler single raw material is probably $3-8k/yr. Total cost of ownership (Ellram's TCO framework) is therefore above the $50.7k price premium.
- **SNP re-pricing and scale loss on the retained two-thirds.** Removing a third of SNP's volume on our recipe reduces their learning and scale (Silbermayr & Minner); the account falls to 0.84% of their revenue. Even a $0.10/lb increase on the retained 45,067 lb would add $4,500/yr. Long-term agreements "will generally impose some kind of take-or-pay or prepayment obligation on the buyer" (Squire Patton Boggs), so any volume we commit to PCI also becomes a liability if forecasts weaken.
- **Relationship signal to SNP.** PVA cooking is SNP's core line and joint hydrogel development is scheduled. Preferred-customer research (Pulles et al. 2016, 91 suppliers) shows suppliers allocate their best resources by customer attractiveness and supplier satisfaction, not volume; a visible loss of confidence works against that at exactly the moment we want first call on SNP's best cook and their innovation.
- **Sub-tier convergence.** If SNP and PCI cook the same producer's PVA resin (resin supply is concentrated among a few producers), PCI hedges SNP's cooking capacity but not the resin. Confirm both suppliers' resin grade and producer before paying any premium.
- **Budget durability.** Redundancy "represents sheer cost with limited benefit unless it is needed due to a disruption" (Sheffi & Rice 2005), and a $50-74k/yr line is a natural target for a future cost push: in McKinsey's 2024 supply-chain survey, 46% of companies were reducing or eliminating risk buffers and board-level supply-risk reporting had fallen from ~50% to 25% *(figures via secondary coverage; plausible)*. A half-funded dual source is worse than a designed backup.
- **Regulatory credit: none.** No regulation or standard requires a second source (section 3.3). Auditors assess proportionate control of each supplier; a second supplier adds a file (and MDSAP auditors explicitly prioritise "newly approved suppliers" for review).

### 2.4 The honest counter-case for Option A

- **"No mitigation" is a decision, not a neutral.** Chopra & Sodhi (2014): "If no risk mitigation strategies are in place for a particular source of disruption, a disruption probability of zero has effectively been assumed"; "Having even two baskets, while adding to the cost, greatly reduces fragility"; and companies must "maintain their commitment to the additional investment (such as a backup supplier) even when no disruption occurs for a few years." Underestimating disruption probability costs more in the long run than overestimating it.
- **Rare-but-long disruptions favour sourcing mitigation, and sometimes standing mitigation.** Tomlin (2006): "sourcing mitigation is increasingly favored over inventory mitigation as disruptions become less frequent but longer" (inventory is impossible for us anyway), and "mitigation rather than contingent rerouting tends to be optimal if disruptions are rare." That result assumes a modest premium and a backup that ramps with lag; at a 300% premium the model's own logic tilts back toward a flexible backup, but the caveat is real: if PCI cannot ramp, a warm backup is weak.
- **It is what most large companies do.** 73% of respondents to McKinsey's 2024 survey reported progress on dual sourcing *(plausible; secondary)*; Deloitte's 2025 CPO survey ranks "maintaining active alternative sources" the most effective mitigation (74%). Note the wording: "active alternative sources," which Option C also satisfies if exercised.
- **The absolute dollars are small relative to the device line.** If a 6-month outage costs $1-3M in lost contribution (section 5), $50k/yr is affordable insurance, and Bain's framing is the right one: decide "how much resilience they need, where it matters most, and what they can afford."
- **Only Option A produces a second source that is live at scale on day one.** Every other option leaves some ramp gap against a 2.5-week TTS.

## 3. Option B: single-source with SNP, strengthened relationship, documented cold-start plan

Sheffi & Rice (2005): "Both approaches are right. The issue is not whether to use a single supplier or multiple ones; the issue is the correct alignment of the corporate-supplier relationship with the procurement strategy. If a company chooses to work with a single supplier, it must develop deep relationships and work very closely with it. Doing otherwise would put the company at great risk." Option B is legitimate only if it is actually done. It is not "keep buying from SNP."

### 3.2 Residual risk after Option B

- **SNP exits (insolvency, sale, owner retirement, fire).** Resin buffer, escrow and playbook shorten TTR but the serial qualification gates remain: realistic TTR still months, not weeks. This is the scenario Option B does not close and that only a ramp-capable second source or in-house capability does. Base rates: established US small businesses show roughly 5-7%/yr exit hazard (Bureau of Labor Statistics survival data; exits include sales, so the failure hazard is lower); SNP-specific factors (age, ownership, succession) dominate.
- **Correlated regional or resin-level events** hit SNP and any second cooker alike; the resin buffer is the hedge.
- **Supplier's TTR optimism.** Philips told customers a one-week delay in 2000; the plant took weeks to restart and months to catch up. Build the playbook on our estimate, not SNP's.
- **Budget and attention decay.** Sheffi & Rice's single-source model requires the deep relationship to be actively maintained; if the forecast sharing and reviews lapse, so does the argument.

### 3.3 What must be documented for QMSR / ISO 13485 defensibility

The FDA Quality Management System Regulation (QMSR; 21 CFR Part 820 incorporating ISO 13485:2016, effective 2 February 2026) contains no purchasing rules of its own; 820.10(a) simply requires a QMS that "complies with the applicable requirements of ISO 13485." ISO 13485 clause 7.4.1 requires supplier criteria based on ability, performance and "the effect of the purchased product on the quality of the medical device," "proportionate to the risk associated with the medical device," plus planned monitoring and re-evaluation. FDA "will not require certificates of conformance to ISO 13485." No regulation, standard or audit model (QMSR, ISO 13485 7.4, GHTF/SG3/N17, NBOG BPG 2010-1, MDSAP, EU MDR Art. 10(9)) requires a second source. The harmonised guidance GHTF/SG3/N17:2008 lists "Continuity of supply" and "Single source suppliers" among business risks, says documenting them is "not a regulatory requirement" but "advisable," and prescribes for a single-source supplier that cannot meet criteria that "the manufacturer should add supplemental controls within his quality management system." FDA's own CDRH Learn training: "If ... you have a sole source supplier and cannot perform active supplier evaluations, then you'll need more emphasis on acceptance activities."

What auditors actually cite is procedural: the Koven Technologies warning letter (21 July 2026) cites failure to establish 7.4.1 criteria, a quality agreement lacking change-notification provisions, and no audits of a supplier the firm's own procedure rated "Highest/Critical Impact." Purchasing controls drew 115 Form 483 observations (4.3%) in FY2025 and appeared in about half of 2025 device warning letters *(secondary statistics)*. Since QMSR, FDA can read supplier audit reports and management-review minutes (the old 820.180(c) exemption is gone), and Compliance Program 7382.850 (30 Jan 2026) lets an investigator follow a complaint into purchasing controls and the risk file.

**Minimum defensible package for Option B, all as controlled records:**
1. SNP supplier risk assessment covering product criticality and business risks per N17 3.1.4 (single source, continuity, financial viability, share of SNP capacity, key person/equipment, sub-tier resin source).
2. Documented control set per N17 3.1.5: audit cadence, CoA verification, strengthened incoming acceptance (viscosity, solids, pH) with periodic third-party check, SPC on lots, change control including sub-tier changes.
3. Signed quality agreement per ISO 13485 7.4.2 / 4.1.5 with the clauses in technique 1 (the PVA cook is an outsourced process).
4. Monitoring and re-evaluation records that match the cadence the procedure promises (USP <1083> recommends supplier performance evaluation at least once a year); do not adopt a "critical supplier" label that promises audits we will not perform (FDA declined to define the term; MDSAP v010 dropped it).
5. The cold-start plan (technique 9) with TTS, TTR estimate, decision triggers and communication steps.
6. A top-management risk-acceptance sign-off (ISO 31000 "retaining the risk by informed decision"), reviewed at every management review with SNP performance and supply-risk status as a standing input.
7. A 506J check: if the device's product code is on FDA's 506J list, an interruption during or in advance of a declared public health emergency (PHE) is a mandatory notification (six months ahead, or within 7 calendar days of an unplanned interruption); outside a PHE notification is voluntary but encouraged.

## 4. Option C: warm / qualified-dormant backup at PCI

### 4.1 Design

- **Finish qualification to production-release status** (same one-time work as Option A: production-scale lot, hydrogel verification, 510(k) change assessment, biocompatibility evaluation, quality agreement, approved supplier list (ASL) entry). Estimate $15-40k internal; much of the lab work is sunk. Qualify PCI at the batch size it would use in a surge; a later scale change is itself a change-control event.
- **Quarterly production buy.** Base case 2 drums (900 lb ≈ 4.8 days of demand) every ~13 weeks, scheduled so they are received, inspected and consumed within the 18-day shelf life. Eight drums/yr = 3,600 lb ≈ 5% of volume. Each buy is an end-to-end drill (receipt, incoming test, hydrogel lot, release) and generates the monitoring record ISO 13485 7.4.1 re-evaluation needs. The cadence is inside the recognised decay points: automotive PPAP requires re-approval after 12 months of inactivity (AIAG rule, by analogy); pharma second-source guidance recommends "at least one small order per quarter" and treats ~18 months idle as "a new supplier again" (vendor guidance).
- **Annual surge drill.** One of the four buys is a full week of demand (3 drums, 1,350 lb) with SNP paused that week, to test rate production and run the hydrogel process on 100% PCI material. Incremental cost ~$1,000/yr.
- **No-fee surge clause in the PCI agreement**: committed lead time to first drum after notice, ramp schedule (e.g., 2 weeks to 50%, 6 weeks to 100%), an execution price formula at volume targeting the ~$1.40/lb market base rather than the $3.00 small-lot price, change notification on resin grade/producer and process, and 12-month rolling forecast. Umbrex's published example is a 70/30 split with the secondary guaranteed a minimum and "surge to 40% upon disruption." A paid capacity-reservation retainer is the wrong instrument at our scale: pharma precedents (Savient-BTG 20% of forecast batch value, forfeited if unused; Oyster Point $2.5M/yr minimum) would imply ~$40,000/yr to reserve full demand at $3/lb.
- **Annual desk re-evaluation, audit every 2-3 years**, CoA trend review each quarter.

### 4.3 What it buys

- TTR falls from 26-52 weeks (cold start) to a ramp of weeks (target 2-6; generic contract-blender first-order lead times of 6-12 weeks show why the surge clause and drill matter). This is nearly Option A's payoff at roughly a quarter of its cost.
- A maintained, legitimately approved supplier with current monitoring data, matching what CPOs call "maintaining active alternative sources."
- Regulatorily it earns no credit; its value is converting a documented risk acceptance (TTR 6-12 months) into a documented mitigation (TTR = PCI ramp).
- Preserves 95% of volume with SNP (account stays ~1.2% of their revenue and grows).

### 4.4 Failure modes

1. **Paper second source.** "A nominal supplier is not fully qualified or exercised; when needed, it cannot ramp" (Umbrex). Tomlin's caveat applies: contingent rerouting works only if the backup has volume flexibility. Mitigation: the surge clause and annual drill; if PCI cannot commit in writing, C is worth little and the choice is A or the bench cook.
2. **Supplier drift.** Personnel, equipment, sub-tier resin grade changes unnoticed. Mitigation: change-notification obligations, quarterly CoA trends.
3. **Ramp gap remains.** Even a working warm backup leaves 0-4 weeks uncovered against TTS 2.5 weeks; pair with Option B's resin buffer and playbook.
4. **We are a nuisance account to PCI too.** $10,800/yr is <1% of any blender above ~$1.1M revenue; PCI's revenue is unknown. Priority must come from the contract and relationship, as with SNP.
5. **Sub-tier convergence.** Same resin producer as SNP means PCI hedges cooking, not resin.
6. **Quiet lapse.** If a buy is skipped for >12 months, treat PCI as needing re-approval and possibly re-validation lots.
7. **Budget erosion.** Book it as a named risk line so it is not cut silently.

## 5. Break-even logic leadership can use

**Rule.** A standing hedge pays when (annual probability of a catastrophic SNP outage, p) x (loss the hedge avoids, L) exceeds its annual premium. Break-even probability p* = premium / L. This is the decision structure of Yu, Zeng & Zhao (2009) and Tomlin (2006), and the real-options view of Chopra & Sodhi (2014), who add that the estimate can be rough but must not be zero: a +/-50% error in p changes long-run cost by <2%, while underestimating is costlier than overestimating.

### 5.1 Break-even annual probability by option

| Loss avoided (L) | $250k | $500k | $1M | $2M | $5M |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Option A at $3.00/lb (premium $50.7k) | 20.3% | 10.1% | **5.1%** | 2.5% | 1.0% |
| Option A if PCI reaches $1.40/lb (premium $14.6k) | 5.8% | 2.9% | 1.5% | 0.7% | 0.3% |
| Option C base (~$12k; range $8-19k scales proportionally) | 4.8% | 2.4% | **1.2%** | 0.6% | 0.2% |

### 5.2 Expected annual loss p x L versus premiums

| p \ L | $250k | $500k | $1M | $2M | $5M |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1%/yr | $2.5k | $5k | $10k | $20k (C pays) | $50k (C pays; A borderline) |
| 2%/yr | $5k | $10k | $20k (C) | $40k (C) | $100k (A and C) |
| 5%/yr | $12.5k (C) | $25k (C) | $50k (C; A borderline) | $100k (A, C) | $250k (A, C) |
| 10%/yr | $25k (C) | $50k (C; A borderline) | $100k (A, C) | $200k (A, C) | $500k (A, C) |

Reading: Option C is justified across almost the whole plausible range; Option A at $3/lb is justified only if a single outage costs $1M or more and we believe SNP's annual catastrophic-failure probability is 5% or higher, or the loss is $2-5M at 1-2.5%. Caveats: this is a one-period approximation; A's avoided loss is only full if PCI ramps inside TTS; C's payoff is slightly smaller (ramp gap); B reduces the outage length rather than avoiding it, so its value is a reduction in L.

**Probability guidance.** McKinsey Global Institute (2020) estimates month-plus disruptions somewhere in a company's supply chain every 3.7 years on average and that a single prolonged production shock erases 30-50% of a year's EBITDA *(plausible; economy-wide modelling, not a per-supplier rate)*. Established US small businesses exit at roughly 5-7%/yr (BLS; includes sales). A 2-5%/yr planning band for a catastrophic SNP outage is defensible absent SNP-specific data; SNP's own age, ownership and succession position should replace it.

### 5.3 What a six-month SNP outage would cost: method (placeholders in brackets)

Components follow the standard downtime cost lists (Siemens True Cost of Downtime 2024; ISO 22301 business impact analysis): lost contribution, idle labour and overhead, emergency-sourcing premium, expedited freight, rework, contractual penalties and lost customers, recovery and qualification spend.

1. Uncovered window W = outage length minus TTS minus bridge coverage. For a 26-week outage: W = 23.5 weeks with no backup; W ≈ ramp minus 2.5 weeks (roughly 0-6 weeks) with a working warm or dual source.
2. Lost contribution = [weekly revenue of every SKU using the hydrogel] x [contribution margin %] x W x [share of missed demand not recovered later; 50-100% for consumables, 20-50% for deferrable demand].
3. Idle cost = weekly direct labour plus allocated overhead of the hydrogel/device line x W.
4. Emergency sourcing premium, if an emergency cooker can be qualified at all: 1,300 lb/week x (~$8.50 - $0.75) ≈ $10-11k/week, ~$250k over a 6-month bridge, using our own CJB quote as the anchor. Rush surcharges of ~20% appear in standard contract clauses; in the 2021 chemical squeeze published prices rose 70-170% (PVC +70%, epoxy +170%, ethylene +43%) with customers on 20-30% allocations.
5. Cold-start qualification under crisis: targeted biocompatibility ~$4-50k, full panels far more *(unverified)*; validation lots; the hours already logged on PCI are the best local estimate; placeholder $75-250k.
6. Commercial and regulatory: failure-to-supply penalties [from contracts], customer notifications, 506J if applicable, long-term share loss (announced disruptions were followed by 6.9% lower sales growth and a ~-40% abnormal stock return over three years; Hendricks & Singhal 2005).

*Illustration only:* a hydrogel-dependent line of $5M/yr revenue at 60% contribution margin, W = 23.5 weeks, 70% unrecovered gives lost contribution ~$0.95M; add idle cost ~$0.1-0.2M, qualification ~$0.15M, emergency premium ~$0.25M: **~$1.4-1.6M**, about 30x the annual PVA spend. At that L, Option A breaks even at p ≈ 3.4%/yr and Option C at ≈ 0.8%/yr.

**What leadership and finance must supply:**
1. Weekly revenue and contribution margin of every SKU that consumes the PVA solution.
2. Share of demand that is deferrable versus lost.
3. Contract penalty and failure-to-supply terms with distributors/GPOs.
4. Hours and spend logged on the PCI qualification to date.
5. PCI's kettle capacity, minimum batch, surge ramp and price at 100% volume (sourcing to obtain).
6. SNP's BCP, financials and succession position (sourcing to obtain under the agreement).
7. Whether the device's product code is on FDA's 506J list.
8. Current CBI insurance wording.

## 6. Evidence from the field

1. **Spend does not predict disruption impact.** Ford/MIT: across 4,534 supplier sites, 2,773 had zero impact and 408 "very high" impact; the impact of a site disruption on lost profit "is not correlated with the amount Ford spends at the supplier"; a two-week disruption at newly identified high-impact sites implied lost revenue "from several hundred thousand dollars to $2.5 billion"; hundreds of suppliers had TTS under one week; the biggest risks often came from "tiny suppliers that provide Ford with components that cost about 10 cents." (Simchi-Levi et al., Interfaces 45(5), 2015; MIT News 2022.)
2. **Redundancy is an insurance premium, and the most disciplined manufacturer chose not to pay it.** Sheffi & Rice (MIT Sloan Management Review 2005): redundancy "represents sheer cost with limited benefit unless it is needed due to a disruption." After the 1997 Aisin fire halted all of Toyota's Japanese plants, Toyota "was back to full production within nine days" through its supplier network and, per Sheffi & Rice, "decided to stay with a single supplier since the cost of having multiple suppliers for the part in question was deemed too high." (The primary case study, Nishiguchi & Beaudet 1998, reports assembly plants reopened after two days and describes continued reliance on Aisin as "unconfirmed reports"; 62 firms directly produced the valves.)
3. **Nokia versus Ericsson was about response, not supplier count.** Both were single-sourced at Philips's Albuquerque plant when it burned in March 2000. Nokia detected the problem early and "was able to find an alternate supply source in three days"; Ericsson lost an estimated $400 million in sales (Sheffi & Rice 2005; Chopra & Sodhi 2004; Chopra & Sodhi 2014 cite a $200M loss in the period) and ceased independent handset manufacturing (Sony Ericsson JV, 2001).
4. **Chemical and medical single-site outages run many months.** Evonik's Marl CDT plant exploded on 31 March 2012 (Evonik and Arkema held about half of world nylon-12 capacity); Evonik first said "at least three months," restart came in early December (~8 months); Arkema put customers on allocations with "more than 50% reduction in supply"; the industry compressed material approvals to 3-4 weeks via an AIAG standardised procedure. Baxter's North Cove plant (about 60% of US IV fluid) was hit by Hurricane Helene on 29 Sept 2024; allocations were removed 13 May 2025 (~7.5 months). GE Healthcare's Omnipaque contrast disruption in 2022 cut one health system's contrast-enhanced CT volume 34% before full capacity returned on 8 June.
5. **Disruptions are financially severe and the probability estimate need not be precise.** Firms announcing supply chain disruptions showed abnormal stock returns of nearly -40% over three years (827 announcements) and, in the year around the glitch, -107% operating income, -6.9% sales growth, +10.7% costs (Hendricks & Singhal 2005). Chopra & Sodhi (2014): having "even two baskets ... greatly reduces fragility"; an error of up to 50% in disruption probability changes total cost by <2%; assuming zero is the expensive mistake.
6. **What procurement leaders actually rate effective.** Deloitte 2025 CPO survey (250+ CPOs): "maintaining active alternative sources" 74%, supply-chain visibility 64%, supplier information sharing and collaboration 61%. Silbermayr & Minner (2016): symmetric splits are never optimal, 75:25 is robust, and the benefit of dual sourcing falls as the incumbent's reliability rises. Bain (2020): "Investing in too much flexibility can render a company uncompetitive."

## 8. Recommendation

**Position:** Stay single-source with SNP and strengthen the relationship now (Option B); finish PCI qualification and hold PCI as a warm, exercised backup (Option C); go to active dual sourcing (Option A) only on defined triggers. The frameworks (Kraljic; Gelderman & van Weele; Sheffi & Rice; Tomlin; Simchi-Levi), the split-ratio and defense-procurement evidence, the supplier-preferencing arithmetic and the break-even table all point the same way: at ~150 drums/yr, a 300% premium on a third of volume buys little that a ~$12k/yr exercised backup plus a ~$5-12k/yr SNP control bundle does not, and it weakens the supplier we depend on most.

**Do now (next 90 days):**
1. Sign the three-year supply/quality agreement with SNP (technique 1) with continuity clauses; share the rolling forecast; hold the scheduled hydrogel brainstorming as a written joint roadmap; assign an executive sponsor.
2. Buy and consign a 12-26-week dry-resin buffer at SNP (~$2.4-5.8k); confirm SNP's resin grade and producer; ask PCI the same.
3. Set up recipe/process escrow (~$2-5k/yr) and run the BCI continuity questionnaire with SNP (key-person cover, second kettle, utility RTO, owner succession).
4. Write the cold-start playbook and the supplier risk assessment; obtain top-management risk acceptance at management review; check 506J status.
5. Finish PCI qualification: production-scale lot, hydrogel verification, 510(k) change assessment, quality agreement with surge clause and change notification; obtain PCI's minimum batch, capacity and price at 100% volume in writing.

**Convert PCI to warm backup when:** PCI passes production-scale verification and the change assessment is filed; PCI's minimum batch is 2 drums or less (or at most 5); and PCI signs a surge commitment with a stated ramp. Then buy 2 drums per quarter with one annual surge-drill week (~$12-19k/yr). If PCI will not commit to a ramp, do not pay for C; cost the in-house bench cook instead.

**Go to active dual sourcing when any of these fires:**
- PCI's qualified price falls to about $1.40/lb or below at a one-third share (premium ≤ ~$15k/yr, i.e., the cost of C).
- Leadership's own disruption-cost estimate and probability put p x L above the A premium (e.g., L ≥ $1M with p ≥ 5%, or L ≥ $2M with p ≥ 2.5%).
- An SNP risk signal: ownership change, distress indicators (Altman Z' < 1.23, current ratio < 1.0, DSO trend), loss of key personnel or capacity, two consecutive quality escapes.
- Annual PVA spend passes roughly $75-100k (year 4-7 at 10% growth, sooner if a second product line adopts the solution), at which point the split ratio and PCI's account both become meaningful.
- The annual surge drill shows PCI cannot reach rate within the committed ramp and SNP's continuity plan has not closed the gap.

**What would change this answer:**
1. **The loss number.** If a six-month outage costs $3M or more in contribution, Option A breaks even at 1.7%/yr, plausibly below SNP's hazard; A becomes rational at today's price.
2. **PCI's price.** At the market base of $1.40/lb the A premium is $14.6k/yr, indistinguishable from C's cost; A would then be the better buy because it removes the ramp gap.
3. **PCI's ramp capability.** If PCI cannot ramp within weeks, C is weak and the choice is A (with confirmed ramp) or in-house capability; if PCI can ramp within TTS, C is nearly as good as A.
4. **SNP's health and succession.** A credible exit risk in the next 2-3 years shifts the decision toward A or the bench cook regardless of cost.
5. **Sub-tier convergence.** If PCI and SNP use the same resin producer, the value of any second cooker falls and the resin buffer plus a second approved resin grade rise in priority.

# Completeness critique

**(1) What is missing**

- **The hybrid nobody priced: "A for 12 months, then C."** Run PCI at 1/3 share for one year (~$50k one-time, not recurring) to *prove* rate production and ramp, then drop to a warm buy. This converts A's biggest weakness (permanent premium) into a bounded qualification cost and fixes C's biggest weakness (unproven ramp). It may be the best option and it is absent.
- **Combined cost of the recommendation.** Leadership is being asked to fund B + C, not C alone: recurring ~$15-31k/yr (C $12-19k + B $3-12k) plus one-time $20-50k (PCI qualification $15-40k + escrow setup + resin buffer), or $50-120k with the bench cook. The memo never adds this up or states a budget ask, owner, or timeline. A CFO will.
- **Product-level consequences of two material streams.** Two PVA sources means hydrogel performance equivalence (not just biocompatibility), lot segregation in production, DHR traceability by source, and more complex complaint/CAPA investigation. R&D and Quality/RA are stakeholders with bandwidth constraints and are not named. This is also a *new risk* A introduces: two suppliers doubles exposure to a supplier-caused quality escape (the Cardinal gown recall is cited but never applied to A).
- **SNP capacity headroom for 10%/yr growth.** The memo models SNP *failure* but not SNP *capacity constraint*: what share of SNP's kettle time do we use, and can they absorb year-5 volume (~99k lb)? If not, a second source becomes a capacity question, not an insurance question.
- **Growth widens the feasible supplier set.** Many of the 94 were eliminated because batch minimums exceed shelf-life-usable volume. At +10%/yr, that constraint loosens every year, which is an argument for *waiting* that the memo does not make.
- **How SNP learns about PCI, and what we tell them.** Options B and C coexist; SNP will discover a qualified backup. The memo asks for SNP's best cook and innovation while quietly qualifying a rival. Needs a transparency script ("QMSR continuity requirement, not a loss of confidence").
- **A full "make" option is dismissed by omission.** Only an emergency bench cook is costed. At $50k/yr spend, in-house production is probably uneconomic, but one line of arithmetic (capex + validation + labor vs spend) would close the question a skeptic will raise.
- **Discounting.** The $309,529 five-year figure is undiscounted and assumes PCI stays at $3.00 for five years, which the memo itself calls above the market high case.

**(2) Weakest points a skeptic will attack**

- **The memo argues against a price it does not believe.** Headline uses $3.00/lb (above the memo's own high case of $2.55); the memo then says the realistic base is $1.40, at which A costs $14.6k and "would be the better buy." A CFO will say the deck is stacked. Present $1.40 base with $3.00 as the conservative sensitivity, or explain why $3.00 is the planning price (the user's instruction) up front.
- **Kraljic cuts the other way.** His bottleneck prescription is "volume insurance (at cost premium if necessary)... backup plans." Read plainly, that endorses paying a premium for a backup, i.e., Option A/C. The memo reinterprets "volume insurance" as an SNP contract clause. Expect the challenge.
- **The Ford "shift more volume" and Toyota/Nokia analogies do not fit.** We are already 100% with SNP, so there is no more volume to shift. Toyota recovered in days because 62 keiretsu firms could improvise; Nokia found alternate capacity because it existed. Our own screen shows 93 of 94 US suppliers cannot cook this. The memo's cases show fast response *requires* an ecosystem we lack, which argues for a pre-positioned backup.
- **Option C's payoff is asserted, not evidenced.** "Nearly Option A's payoff at a quarter of its cost" rests entirely on PCI signing a no-fee surge clause with a 2-6-week ramp to 1,300 lb/week. A blender buying 8 drums/yr from us has no incentive to reserve that capacity for free; a supplier at 5% of volume has never demonstrated rate. The memo's own failure mode 1 ("paper second source") is the base case, not an edge case.
- **TTS of 2.5 weeks is generous.** With weekly deliveries and drums already aged on receipt, effective TTS is ~1-1.5 weeks. Every ramp-gap calculation (A: 2-6 weeks; C: 0-4 weeks) understates exposure by about a week.
- **The probability band is borrowed.** 2-5%/yr comes from BLS all-small-business exits (which include sales), not from anything SNP-specific. Recommending "accept a 23-50-week stoppage" on a borrowed p and a placeholder L is the memo's soft underbelly; the recommendation should be explicitly conditional on the loss number arriving.
- **Supplier-preferencing arithmetic is false precision.** The 1% "nuisance line" is a practitioner heuristic; 1.27% vs 0.84% is not a meaningful difference. Worse, PVA cooking is SNP's *core line*, so our share of that line is far above 1.27% of total revenue; the memo should compute share-of-line, which likely strengthens the argument for keeping volume whole.

**(3) Internal inconsistencies**

- **Option C is priced four ways.** Bottom line: "roughly $12,000"; Section 4.2 base: $12-19k; Section 7: "$6-12k... remains accurate"; 1-drum row: $8-15k. Section 7's own components ($4-8k premium + $1-3k testing + $3-8k management) sum to $8-19k, not $6-12k. The break-even table uses the $12k floor. Pick one number and its range and use it everywhere; the July continuity argument currently fails on the memo's own table.
- **Asymmetric overhead treatment.** A's break-even uses $50.7k price premium only (excluding the $3-8k dual-QC overhead the memo itself identifies); C's cost includes $3-8k management. Either include both or neither.
- **Trigger arithmetic.** "Annual PVA spend passes $75-100k (year 4-7 at 10% growth)": $50.7k × 1.1^n crosses $75k at year 5 (n=4: $74.2k) and $100k at year 8 (n=7: $98.8k). Should read "year 5-8."
- **Option B bundle** is "$3-12k/yr" in Section 3.1 and "$5-12k/yr" in Section 8.
- **Surge drill vs SNP commitment.** Technique 1 commits ~150 drums/yr to SNP with take-or-pay; the annual surge drill "pauses SNP that week." Small, but a lawyer will notice.
- **$309,529 five-year figure** uses $3.00 flat, contradicting the memo's argument that PCI's price should fall toward $1.40 as it matures.

**(4) The single most decisive missing datum**

Weekly contribution margin of every SKU that consumes the hydrogel (L). It decides whether $50k/yr is material at all: at L = $500k, A needs p ≥ 10% (indefensible); at L = $3M, A breaks even at 1.7% (plausibly below SNP's hazard) and the recommendation flips to A at today's assumed price. (Close second, and more obtainable: PCI's written price at 1/3 share and committed ramp, which flips A vs C on its own; sourcing can get this in two weeks.)

How to ask: give FP&A a three-column table, not a question. "For each SKU using the PVA hydrogel: trailing-12-month revenue; standard contribution margin %; share of demand customers would defer vs lose permanently in a 6-month stockout." Fallback if they will not commit: "Is a 6-month stop more or less than $1M in lost contribution? More or less than $3M?" Two yes/no answers place us on the break-even table.

**(5) Three questions leadership will ask, with the memo's best answers**

1. *"In July you said $6-12k a year. Now it's $50k. Which is it?"*
   Both, for different products. $6-12k (properly $8-19k) buys a qualified backup exercised with 1-2 drums a quarter. $50k buys an active one-third split at $3.00/lb, a price above the market high case; at the $1.40 market base, the same split costs $14.6k. The July number is still right for the backup design; we now know what the active version costs, and we are recommending the backup.

2. *"If SNP burns down tomorrow, what actually happens under your plan?"*
   Today: production stops for roughly 23-50 weeks. Under B+C once PCI is qualified with a signed ramp: a gap of ~0-4 weeks, shortened by the resin buffer and cold-start playbook. Under A: a gap of ~2-6 weeks unless PCI can surge to 100% inside our shelf-life window, which we have not confirmed. No option closes the gap fully without PCI's written capacity commitment or in-house bridge capability, which is why the first action is to get that commitment in writing.

3. *"Why not just pay the $50k? It's rounding error against the device line."*
   Because at $3.00 it buys coverage for one-third of supply, not all of it; it drops us below 1% of SNP's revenue at the moment we want first call on their capacity and innovation; and it is a standing line that survey data say gets cut in the next budget cycle. Break-even at $3.00 needs a single outage costing $1M+ with a 5%+ annual probability. The warm backup captures most of the same protection at roughly a quarter of the cost, and we have written triggers (PCI price ≤ $1.40, SNP distress signal, spend > $75k, loss estimate above the premium) that convert to active dual sourcing the moment the arithmetic changes.
