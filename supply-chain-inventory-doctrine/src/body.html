The Inventory
Doctrine What the theory said before 2019, what 2020 did to it, and why the KPIs changed

For a century, inventory theory pointed one way: hold less. The models were rigorous, the finance was sound, and they all rested on a single assumption that nobody wrote down — that supply shows up. Between 2020 and 2022 that assumption failed in every direction at once. What followed was not a swing to "just in case." It was something more interesting, and the measurement system is where you can see it.

01The doctrine1913 – 2019

A century of theory, all pointing the same way

Pre-COVID inventory theory was not sloppy. It was some of the most rigorous applied mathematics in management, and it was almost unanimous: inventory is capital in a warehouse, it is the physical residue of an information failure, and the correct amount of it is the smallest amount that meets your service level. Here is the canon that operators were trained on.

F. W. Harris · 1913

Economic Order Quantity

Trade the fixed cost of placing an order against the cost of holding what it brings. Differentiate, solve, get one number.

Told you toOrder in the arithmetically optimal lot. Anything larger is a financing decision you did not mean to make.

Edgeworth 1888 · formalised 1951

The newsvendor model

One order, uncertain demand. Stock up to the quantile where the cost of one more unit equals the cost of one too few.

Told you toDerive stock from a service level, and the service level from a cost ratio — not from nerves. Edgeworth posed it for bank cash reserves; Arrow, Harris and Marschak gave it its modern form.

Statistical inventory control · 1950s

Safety stock & reorder point

Buffer the demand variation that can occur during the replenishment lead time, scaled by the service factor z.

Told you toHold z·σD·√L and no more. This is the formula that shipped inside essentially every ERP system.

Taiichi Ohno · Toyota · 1950s–1988

Just-in-Time / TPS

Inventory is the first of the seven wastes. Lower the water in the pond and the rocks — long changeovers, bad quality, unreliable suppliers — become visible and get fixed.

Told you toTreat stock as a symptom, never as insurance. Removing it is how you find out what is actually wrong.

Forrester 1961 · Lee, Padmanabhan & Whang 1997

The bullwhip effect

Order variance amplifies at every echelon upstream. Four causes: demand signal processing, order batching, price fluctuation, rationing and shortage gaming.

Told you toFix amplification with information, not stock. Share the point-of-sale signal and the buffer becomes unnecessary.

Gary Eppen · 1979

Risk pooling & the square-root law

Consolidate n stocking locations into one and the safety stock needed falls by roughly √n — a 68% reduction going from ten locations to one.

Told you toCentralise. Fewer, bigger nodes. Which is also, exactly, how you build a single point of failure.

Lee & Billington · HP · c.1993

Postponement

Hold inventory in generic form and differentiate as late as possible, so one pooled buffer serves many end variants. Moving DeskJet localisation from the factory to the distribution centres cut finished-goods inventory investment 21% at a 98% fill rate.

Told you toPush the decoupling point downstream. Still good advice — it survived the decade intact.

Eliyahu Goldratt · 1984

Theory of Constraints

Throughput is set by one bottleneck. Buffer that, subordinate everything else, and stock held anywhere else is pure carrying cost.

Told you toConcentrate protection at one known point — on the assumption that you know where the constraint is.

Womack & Jones · 1996

Lean thinking

Specify value, map the value stream, make it flow, let the customer pull, pursue perfection. Inventory is a failure of flow.

Told you toConvert push to pull everywhere, and read any accumulation of stock as a defect in the system.

Marshall Fisher · HBR · 1997

Functional vs innovative products

Stable, predictable products need a physically efficient chain; volatile, short-life products need a market-responsive one. Fisher quantified the split: 10% forecast error and 1–2% stockouts for functional goods, against 40–100% error and 10–40% stockouts for innovative ones.

Told you toMatch the chain to the product. For most industrial goods that meant: minimise cost, hold as little as possible.

Hau Lee · HBR · 2004

The Triple-A supply chain

Speed and cost are not enough. A chain must be agile, adaptable and aligned to survive. Drawn from more than 60 companies studied over 15 years.

Told you toBuild for change — sixteen years early. Widely cited, thinly implemented, because none of the three had a number attached.

Corporate finance · throughout

The cash conversion cycle

CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO. Inventory is invested capital sitting in the denominator of return on capital.

Told you toCut days of inventory, because it is the fastest lever on free cash flow — and this is the one your bonus was actually tied to.

The unwritten assumption

Every model above treats uncertainty as something that happens on the demand side. Supply is a pipe: it has a length, and the length is a constant.

Read the formulas again and you can see it. Demand gets a distribution — a mean, a standard deviation, a service factor. Lead time gets a letter. Not a distribution: a letter, L, a fixed number of days you look up in a table. Suppliers were assumed available, ports were assumed open, and capacity was assumed purchasable at a price. This was not naive. For roughly thirty years, between the opening of global trade and 2020, it was empirically true nearly all of the time — which is precisely why nobody stress-tested it.

The five equations that ran the warehouse

Four of them are about physical goods. The fifth is the one that determined what anyone was actually paid to do.

The warnings that were already published

It is not true that nobody saw this coming. The resilience literature pre-dates COVID by fifteen years and it was accurate. It failed to change behaviour for a reason that is itself a measurement problem: the cost of resilience is certain, immediate and lands in one budget, while the benefit is probabilistic, deferred and lands in someone else's. There was no line on the P&L called "disruption that did not happen."

Chopra & Sodhi · MIT SMR · 2004
Separated recurrent risk (demand noise, which inventory handles) from disruptive risk (which it mostly does not), and warned that mitigation bought for one increases exposure to the other.
Christopher & Peck · 2004
Defined supply chain resilience as a design property — re-engineering, collaboration, agility, risk culture — rather than a stock level.
Yossi Sheffi · The Resilient Enterprise · 2005
Gave the disruption profile its shape: detection, first response, full impact, recovery. Argued flexibility beats redundancy because you use it every day rather than only in a crisis.
Hendricks & Singhal · 2003–05
Showed disruptions are not transient accounting events: announcing one was associated with large, persistent declines in shareholder value and operating performance that did not recover quickly.
02What actually broke2020 – 2022

Not a shortage. A failure of the assumption underneath the shortage

It is tempting to summarise 2020–22 as "there was not enough stuff." That is not what the data shows, and it is not the lesson. What broke was the premise that supply is a constant — and it broke in a correlated way, everywhere at once, which is the one failure mode none of the models could represent.

The sequence

Feb – Apr 2020
Supply stops, then demand inverts
Chinese production halts. Weeks later, spending rotates violently out of services and into goods. Two shocks with opposite signs arrive inside one quarter.
Total inventories-to-sales spikes to 1.74 in April — a sales collapse, not a stock build
Mid 2020
The bullwhip, textbook edition
Carmakers, reading a recession, cancel semiconductor orders. Foundries reallocate that capacity to consumer electronics, where demand is exploding. When autos return, the queue is full and automotive chips are the least profitable thing a fab can make.
Lee, Padmanabhan & Whang described this exact mechanism in 1997
Mar 2021
Ever Given grounds in the Suez Canal
Six days and seven hours of blockage. At least 369 ships were queued by 28 March and over 400 by the 29th; the backlog cleared on 3 April. A demonstration that a single geographic chokepoint sits inside thousands of bills of material that never named it.

Jun 2021
US retail runs out of inventory
The retail inventories-to-sales ratio falls to 1.09 — the lowest reading in a series that begins in 1992. Retail had spent thirty years proving it could operate on less stock, and then found out what the floor was.
1.09 — down from a 1.46 average in 2019
Sep 2021
The chip shortage gets a price tag
AlixPartners revises its forecast of 2021 revenue lost to the semiconductor shortage from $110bn to $210bn, and unbuilt vehicles from 3.9m to 7.7m — a near-doubling in four months, which is itself the story: nobody could size the exposure. The measured outturn was worse still. S&P Global Mobility later put actual 2021 light-vehicle production lost to the shortage at more than 9.5m units.

Dec 2021 – Jan 2022
Peak pressure
The GSCPI reaches +4.44σ. On 9 January 2022 a record 109 container ships are waiting for berths at Los Angeles and Long Beach. Ocean freight, port capacity, chips, labour and warehousing are constrained simultaneously — the correlated failure the models assumed away.

2020 – 2022
Lead time stops being a number
The single most important series for this document, and the one almost nobody was tracking. Susquehanna's semiconductor lead-time survey runs from 13.9 weeks in autumn 2020 to a record 27.1 weeks in May 2022 — and stays there. Not a spike: a doubling that held for two years. Every safety-stock calculation in every planning system was parameterised on the first number.

Spring 2022
Shanghai locks down
Two years in, the single-country concentration built over two decades is still there, and still binding.
May – Jun 2022
The whiplash reverses: the glut
Target ends its Q1 with $15.1bn of inventory, 43% above the prior year, cuts full-year guidance on 18 May and cuts it again three weeks later. Walmart is up 33%. Firms that had spent 18 months being punished for holding too little are now punished for holding too much.

May 2023

The GSCPI falls to −1.59σ. Freight rates collapse, destocking runs through the year, and the resilience budgets raised in 2021 meet the cost-cutting cycle of 2023.
2025 – 2026
A different shock, on the same fault lines
Tariff changes and re-sourcing push the index back to +1.84σ in April 2026 — not a 2021-scale event, but a second live test of whether the capabilities built after 2020 were kept.

Four places the models were wrong — and one place the incentives were

Each of these maps directly onto a metric that exists today and did not exist, or was not tracked, in 2019.

01 · Uncertainty was on the wrong side
Demand got a distribution; supply got a constant. Every buffer in the network was sized for the variance that mattered least in 2021.
02 · Failures were assumed independent
Risk pooling, portfolio diversification and dual sourcing all rest on independence. Two suppliers on the same island, feeding through the same port, buying from the same tier three, are one supplier wearing two names.
03 · The process was assumed stationary
Forecasts and safety stocks are refit on history. When the data-generating process changes, history stops being evidence — and every model retrained on 2020–21 then carried the shock forward into 2023 as if it were signal.
04 · The map stopped at tier one
You cannot buffer, dual-source or insure a node you cannot name. Most firms discovered their critical dependency only when it stopped.
05 · And the scorecard was paying for all of it
This is the part that is not a modelling error. Purchase price variance rewarded the buyer who consolidated volume onto the cheapest single source. Asset utilisation rewarded the plant with no slack. Inventory turns rewarded the planner who cut the buffer, and reported nothing when the stockout arrived. Cash-to-cash improved when you pushed inventory onto a supplier who financed it worse than you did. Every one of these metrics was going the right way in 2019. The fragility was not a failure to hit the targets. It was the targets.
The formula, written out in full — the version the textbooks always had (Hadley & Whitin; carried into practice via Silver, Pyke & Peterson, and King 2009) SS = z · √( E[L] · σD² + E[D]² · σL² ) ↑ ↑ demand variance lead-time variance — set to zero by assumption With σL = 0 this collapses to the familiar SS = z · σD · √L. Note the asymmetry: demand variance is multiplied by the mean lead time, but lead-time variance is multiplied by the SQUARE of mean demand. On a high-volume part, a few weeks of lead-time uncertainty outweighs everything the demand term can contribute.

Four compounding reasons the buffer was too small

The zeroed term is the famous one, but it was not acting alone. Each of these is independent of the others, and in 2021 all four fired at once.

01 · σL was estimated from a calm regime
Even where firms did estimate supply variability, they estimated it from history — and the history was quiet. The GSCPI's standard deviation across 1998–2019 was 0.494, and its maximum over those 22 years was +1.58. December 2021 came in at +4.44: nine times that standard deviation, and 2.8× the entire pre-2020 range. No estimator fed on that sample could have sized what followed.
02 · the term is quadratic in mean demand
Because σL is multiplied by mean demand squared, it takes over the variance budget fast. On a representative high-volume part, lead-time variance is already 77% of total variance at three days of lead-time noise and 98% at twelve. The term that was assumed away is the term that dominates.
03 · the shocks were correlated, and the formula assumes they are not
The expression above adds two variances because it assumes demand and lead time are independent. In 2021 they were positively correlated — the same congestion that stretched lead times was driven by the same demand surge — so there is an omitted covariance term, and omitting it biases the buffer downward exactly when you need it most.
04 · z assumes normality, and tails are not normal
The service factor z converts a service level into a multiplier only if lead-time demand is normally distributed. For a merely lognormal distribution — mildly right-skewed, nothing exotic — the true buffer is +15% at the 95th percentile, +45% at the 99th and +85% at the 99.9th. The higher the service level you promised, the worse the under-sizing.
And a large part of the 2021 build was not a decision at all. Little's Law — L = λW, inventory equals throughput times flow time — is a distribution-free identity, not a behavioural theory. If throughput holds steady and end-to-end flow time goes from 8 weeks to 20, pipeline inventory rises 2.5× whether anyone decided anything or not. A great deal of what was reported in 2021 as firms "moving to just-in-case" was arithmetic: the same orders, in transit for longer. It is worth knowing which part of an inventory increase is a strategy and which part is a queue.
03The correction2022 – 2026

The answer was not "hold more." It was "hold differently"

The popular account is that just-in-time died and just-in-case replaced it. The measured data says something sharper. Two sectors that entered 2019 holding almost exactly the same inventory relative to sales have ended up in opposite places — and the split tells you precisely which parts of the new doctrine were real.

Where the buffer stayed

Manufacturers hold more than their 2015–19 average, and have done consistently since 2021. This is the sector with single-sourced, long-lead, low-value, line-stopping components — the $3 connector that idles a $40,000 vehicle. For that class of part the economics of a buffer were always overwhelming and simply were not being calculated, because the aggregate metric a plant was judged on could not tell a $3 connector from a pallet of finished goods.

Where it did not

Retail is leaner than before COVID on every baseline — 1.25 against a 2015–19 average of 1.47. It went from 1.09 and empty shelves, through a glut that produced billions in markdowns, to a position tighter than the one it started from. Nothing about retail's exposure profile rewards a permanent buffer: the goods are substitutable, the lead times shorter, and holding the wrong seasonal stock costs more than running out of it.

This is the actual finding. "Just-in-case replaced just-in-time" is false at the aggregate level — total US business inventories-to-sales sits 0.10 below its 2015–19 average, and wholesalers and retail are further below still. The doctrine that replaced lean was not "hold more." It was hold according to consequence: heavier buffers on a small number of parts that can stop a line, thinner buffers than ever on everything else. That is a change in the composition of inventory, which is exactly why it needed new metrics — the old ones could only measure the level.
Where this finding is fragile, and where it is not. Honesty about the baseline matters here, because manufacturing inventories were already climbing through 2019 under the trade war — from 1.40 in January to 1.52 in December. So the manufacturing result moves with the baseline you choose: +0.08 against the 2015–19 average, +0.03 against the 2019 average, and −0.04 against December 2019 alone. Read against that single month, manufacturers hold marginally less, not more.

What does not move is the rest of it. Retail, wholesale and the total are below every baseline. And the divergence between manufacturing and retail — the thing that actually evidences buffering by consequence — is robust and large under all three: the gap between the two sectors widened by +0.29, +0.24 and +0.14 months respectively. The two sectors moved apart. That is the claim to hang weight on, not the size of manufacturing's own increase.
04The new doctrine2022 –

Nine things that replaced "minimise inventory"

None of these ideas are new. Almost all of them existed in the literature before 2019. What changed is that they acquired numbers, owners and budget — which is the difference between a framework and a practice.

Replaces ABC-by-spend

Segment by consequence

ABC classification ranked parts by annual purchase value, so the cheap ones got the least attention.

NowRank by what happens if it stops: sole-sourced, long re-qualification, no substitute, line-stopping. The central empirical result of the Ford risk-exposure work is that exposure is uncorrelated with procurement spend — low-value single-sourced fasteners and sensors repeatedly carried the highest impact, while the parts finance watched carried little.

Ptak & Smith · DDMRP

Position the buffer, don't just size it

A single end-to-end forecast transmits every error along the whole chain.

NowInsert decoupling points and hold buffers there, so variability is absorbed at chosen places instead of propagating. The question moved from "how much" to "where".

Simchi-Levi, Schmidt & Wei · HBR 2014

Measure impact, not probability

You cannot estimate the annual probability of a fire at a specific plant, and pretending otherwise produced risk registers full of invented numbers.

NowAssume the node is down. Measure time-to-recover and time-to-survive, and price the gap in revenue. It is auditable, and it is arguable in front of a CFO.

Resilinc · Interos · Everstream

n-tier visibility as a coverage number

Dual-sourcing tier one is worthless if both tier ones buy from the same tier three.

NowPercentage of critical BOM mapped to tier n becomes a tracked coverage KPI with a target, not a one-off consulting exercise.

Clark–Scarf · Graves & Willems

Multi-echelon optimisation

Setting safety stock site by site guarantees the network holds more in total than it needs, in the wrong places.

NowOptimise across echelons at once. The theory dates to 1960; commodity compute and clean data are what made it default practice after 2021.

M5 competition · 2020

Distributions, not point forecasts

A single expected-demand number plus a MAPE target tells you nothing about the tail you actually need to buffer.

NowForecast quantiles directly and score them with pinball loss. In M5's probabilistic track — 42,840 Walmart series, nine quantiles — the winner beat the best statistical benchmark by 24.7%. The inventory decision needs the 95th percentile, so forecast the 95th percentile.

Network design

China+1, regional, nearer

Two decades of single-country concentration turned a cost optimum into a correlated exposure.

NowQualified alternates in a second geography, regional service for regional demand. Genuinely under way — and, as §07 shows, considerably slower than the survey answers suggest.

Commercial terms

Buy capacity, not only goods

Inventory is one way to buy the right to supply later. It is not the only way, and often not the cheapest.

NowCapacity reservation, take-or-pay and multi-year agreements — an option premium paid in cash instead of warehouse space. Cirrus Logic's 2021 wafer agreement with GlobalFoundries priced it explicitly: $60m of non-refundable fees against ~$1.6bn of committed purchases — a 3.75% premium.

Industrial policy

The state became a supply chain actor

For thirty years, sourcing was a commercial decision. It is now partly a regulated one.

NowSubsidy, export control, forced-labour import bans and tariffs change the landed cost and the legality of a source. Sourcing decisions carry a policy risk line that did not exist in the 2019 model.

The steel-man for just-in-time. It is worth saying plainly that JIT did not cause this, and the better analysts never claimed it did. Small batch sizes and short cycle times did not create the semiconductor shortage; a cancelled order book, a two-decade concentration of capacity and zero visibility below tier one did. Toyota — the originator — weathered the chip shortage better than most of its competitors precisely because it had a risk-part database and multi-month buffers on specific critical components, a policy it adopted after the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake. That is not an abandonment of TPS. It is TPS applied to a risk that had been made visible. The lesson is not "lean was wrong." It is "lean was being measured with instruments that could not see supply-side risk." The sharpest academic statement of this comes from Jiang, Rigobon and Rigobon, who argue that under a systemic shock — as opposed to the idiosyncratic ones the models were built for — neither just-in-time nor just-in-case is the right frame at all, and the optimal policy is closer to "just in worst case": diversify the sourcing network rather than deepen the pile.
05The KPI ledger16 metric pairs

Every KPI that changed, and the exact reason it had to

This is the argument of the whole document, repeated sixteen times. Read each row left to right: the metric that governed the decision before 2019, what it was optimising for, the failure mode 2020–22 exposed in it, and the metric that now sits beside or in place of it. Almost none of the old metrics were wrong. They were incomplete in one specific, identifiable way — and the new metric is the patch for that specific hole.

The pattern across all sixteen. Every old metric is an aggregate, a point estimate, or a cost. Every new one is a decomposition, a distribution, or an exposure. That is the whole shift in one line: the pre-2019 system measured the average of a stable world in a single number, and the post-2022 system measures the shape of an unstable one — because the average is exactly what tells you nothing during a shock.
06Risk assessmentThe conceptual shifts

Seven changes underneath all of it

Strip away the individual metrics and the same seven moves sit underneath every one of them. These are the ones worth arguing about, because they determine whether a firm has genuinely changed its model of the world or has simply bought some software.

01Uncertainty is on the demand side→Uncertainty is on both sides

The single most consequential change. Every classical model gives demand a distribution and supply a constant. The post-2020 model gives lead time, yield, capacity and availability their own distributions — which means the buffer calculation, the forecast and the network design all have to be redone, not re-parameterised.

02Risk = probability × impact→Risk = impact, failure assumed

The probability of a specific plant failing in a specific year is not knowable, and every register that claimed otherwise was recording a guess dressed as an estimate. Assuming failure and measuring recovery time removes the fabricated input entirely. It is a weaker claim about the world and a much stronger basis for a decision.

03Failures are independent→Failures are correlated

Risk pooling, portfolio diversification and dual sourcing all rest on independence — and independence is exactly what a shared tier-three supplier, a shared port, a shared country or a shared cloud region destroys. Most of what looks like new technology in this field, from n-tier mapping to network digital twins, exists to find correlation that the org chart hides.

04Inventory is a level→Inventory is a portfolio

A single number to be minimised became a set of positions with different jobs: cycle stock buys efficiency, safety stock buys service, strategic stock buys time, and excess and obsolete stock is a loss you have not booked yet. They are managed by different people against different targets, and aggregating them destroyed the only information that mattered.

05Forecast the expected value→Forecast the distribution

An inventory decision has always been a quantile decision — the service factor z in the safety-stock formula is a quantile. Pairing a point forecast with an assumed normal distribution was a computational convenience from an era without compute. Forecasting the quantile directly, and scoring it as a quantile, closes a gap that was always there.

06Minimise local cost→Maximise network survivability

The objective function itself changed. It was: minimise cost subject to a service level. It is now: minimise total cost including the expected cost of disruption, subject to surviving a defined set of node failures. The constraint is new — and a new constraint is a genuinely different problem, not a different weighting of the same one.

07Risk lives in a register→Risk lives on the balance sheet

The change that made the other six fundable. As long as exposure was a colour on a heat map, it lost every budget argument to a cost saving denominated in currency. Expressing it as revenue-at-risk put it on the same terms as everything else a capital committee compares — which is the only reason any of this outlived the crisis that prompted it.

And the part that did not change at all

A document like this one has an obvious failure mode: making it sound as though a century of operations research was overturned in 2020. It was not. The decision rules are intact. What changed is what you feed them.

The newsvendor is untouched
Stock to Q* = F⁻¹(cu / (cu + co)) — still exactly right. The entire probabilistic-forecasting shift is best understood as replacing the estimator of F: from "normal, with a standard deviation plugged in from history" to a directly forecast empirical distribution. The rule did not move. The input did.
EOQ is untouched, and forgiving
Q* = √(2DK/h) still balances ordering against holding cost, and it is famously insensitive to getting the inputs wrong: a 100% error in demand produces only a 41% error in the order quantity. It was never the fragile part of the system.
Little's Law is not a theory
L = λW is an identity. It holds in every regime, requires no distributional assumption, and quietly explains a great deal of what was reported in 2021 as a change of strategy. Lengthen the pipe and it fills, whatever anyone intended.
Nearly everything in §04 and §05 predates COVID, often by decades — Clark and Scarf's multi-echelon decomposition is from 1960, Little's Law from 1961, proper scoring rules for probabilistic forecasts were settled long before the M5 competition demonstrated them at scale, and conditional value-at-risk became tractable in 2000. Adoption was the bottleneck, not theory. What 2020–22 supplied was not new mathematics. It was the first argument in thirty years that a CFO would accept for paying the cost of using the mathematics that already existed.
07Did it stick?2023 – 2026

What leaders say, and what the data says

Any honest version of this document has to answer the sceptic's question: was this a genuine change of doctrine, or a crisis-era enthusiasm that faded when the cost programmes came back? Survey evidence and measured evidence disagree sharply here, and the disagreement is the most useful thing in the document. Where they conflict, this section believes the measured series.

The honest verdict

Retreated · intent peaked in 2022
Dual sourcing fell from 81% to 73% and regionalisation from 64% to 60% between 2022 and 2024. McKinsey's own reading is that momentum has plateaued. The 2023 cost-cutting cycle arrived before most resilience programmes had finished paying for themselves.
Retreated · the 2022 glut was not precision
Target at +43% and Walmart at +33% year on year were not firms executing a considered buffer strategy. They were firms that ordered aggressively into a demand signal that had already turned — the bullwhip again, in the opposite direction, eighteen months later.
Retreated · resilience often meant software
Visibility platforms and control towers are easier to buy than second sources are to qualify, and they book as one project rather than a permanent cost increase. A dashboard showing an exposure you have not funded a response to is not resilience.
Retreated · the largest firms reverted completely
The clearest single result in the measured evidence. Among 471 large SEC filers, the biggest third finished 2025 on 47.7 inventory days against 48.7 in 2019. Whatever they added in 2021, they gave back. The firms that buy the most consulting advice on resilience are the ones whose balance sheets show the least of it.
Stuck · but in the middle of the distribution
The mid-size third of the same panel went from 81.5 to 90.6 days and held it — an 11% increase that survived four years and two cost cycles. Smaller firms have less pricing power with suppliers, fewer qualified alternates and thinner logistics options, so for them the buffer is the only available instrument. That is a rational, durable response, and the aggregate hides it completely.
Stuck · physical and legal facts
Fabs and plants that were built are still built. Industrial policy, export controls and origin rules are still law. Long-term capacity agreements still run. Manufacturing still holds more inventory relative to sales than it did in 2019, five years on.
Stuck · the measurement system
This is the durable part, and it is why the KPI ledger is the centre of this document. Metrics outlive the enthusiasm that created them, because they are embedded in systems, reports and comp plans. A firm that added revenue-at-risk to its planning review in 2021 is still running it.
The live test, now
Pressure is back at +1.84σ as of April 2026. And the 2025 tariff front-run produced the most violent inventory swing in the US national accounts outside the pandemic itself: the change in private inventories added +2.58pp to real GDP growth in Q1 2025 and subtracted −3.44pp in Q2 — a 6.0-point reversal in two quarters. That is a bullwhip, in 2025, in firms that had spent four years saying they had fixed the bullwhip. It is the clearest evidence in this document that the measurement changes described in §05 have not yet changed behaviour at scale.

Where this document is weak. Five things a careful reader should hold against it. One: the manufacturing result is baseline-sensitive. Inventories in that sector were already climbing through 2019, so "manufacturers hold more than before COVID" is true against a multi-year pre-COVID average and false against December 2019 alone. §03 shows all three baselines rather than picking the flattering one; the robust claim is the divergence between manufacturing and retail, not the size of manufacturing's own increase. Two: sector-level ratios average over enormous firm-level variation — a sector can be flat while half its firms doubled their buffers and the other half halved them. Three: the survey waves are not a matched panel, so the year-on-year movements in §07 are indicative rather than measured change. Four: the KPI ledger describes practice at large, well-resourced firms; the median manufacturer has adopted far less of it than the literature implies. Five: several framework attributions are conventional rather than uncontested — priority disputes exist for the EOQ formula and for parts of the bullwhip literature, and the dates given are for the canonical publication, not the first idea.

If you take one thing from this: the pre-2019 system was not badly run. It was well run against an incomplete scorecard, and the scorecard's blind spot was supply-side uncertainty. Everything in §05 is an attempt to fix that one hole. Whether it holds depends less on what firms believe than on whether the numbers are still being produced in three years — which is the only reliable test of whether a doctrine actually changed.
