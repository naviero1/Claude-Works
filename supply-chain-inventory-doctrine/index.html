The Inventory
Doctrine What the theory said before 2019, what 2020 did to it, and why the KPIs changed

For a century, inventory theory pointed one way: hold less. The models were rigorous, the finance was sound, and they all rested on a single assumption that nobody wrote down — that supply shows up. Between 2020 and 2022 that assumption failed in every direction at once. What followed was not a swing to "just in case." It was something more interesting, and the measurement system is where you can see it.

01The doctrine1913 – 2019

A century of theory, all pointing the same way

Pre-COVID inventory theory was not sloppy. It was some of the most rigorous applied mathematics in management, and it was almost unanimous: inventory is capital in a warehouse, it is the physical residue of an information failure, and the correct amount of it is the smallest amount that meets your service level. Here is the canon that operators were trained on.

F. W. Harris · 1913

Economic Order Quantity

Trade the fixed cost of placing an order against the cost of holding what it brings. Differentiate, solve, get one number.

Told you toOrder in the arithmetically optimal lot. Anything larger is a financing decision you did not mean to make.

Arrow · Harris · Marschak · 1951

The newsvendor model

One order, uncertain demand. Stock up to the quantile where the cost of one more unit equals the cost of one too few.

Told you toDerive stock from a service level, and derive the service level from a cost ratio — not from nerves.

Statistical inventory control · 1950s

Safety stock & reorder point

Buffer the demand variation that can occur during the replenishment lead time, scaled by the service factor z.

Told you toHold z·σD·√L and no more. This is the formula that shipped inside essentially every ERP system.

Taiichi Ohno · Toyota · 1950s–1988

Just-in-Time / TPS

Inventory is the first of the seven wastes. Lower the water in the pond and the rocks — long changeovers, bad quality, unreliable suppliers — become visible and get fixed.

Told you toTreat stock as a symptom, never as insurance. Removing it is how you find out what is actually wrong.

Forrester 1961 · Lee, Padmanabhan & Whang 1997

The bullwhip effect

Order variance amplifies at every echelon upstream. Four causes: demand signal processing, order batching, price fluctuation, rationing and shortage gaming.

Told you toFix amplification with information, not stock. Share the point-of-sale signal and the buffer becomes unnecessary.

Gary Eppen · 1979

Risk pooling & the square-root law

Consolidate n stocking locations into one and the safety stock needed falls by roughly √n.

Told you toCentralise. Fewer, bigger nodes. Which is also exactly how you build a single point of failure.

Lee & Billington · HP · c.1993

Postponement

Hold inventory in generic form and differentiate as late as possible, so one pooled buffer serves many end variants.

Told you toPush the decoupling point downstream. Still good advice — it survived the decade intact.

Eliyahu Goldratt · 1984

Theory of Constraints

Throughput is set by one bottleneck. Buffer that, subordinate everything else, and stock held anywhere else is pure carrying cost.

Told you toConcentrate protection at one known point — on the assumption that you know where the constraint is.

Womack & Jones · 1996

Lean thinking

Specify value, map the value stream, make it flow, let the customer pull, pursue perfection. Inventory is a failure of flow.

Told you toConvert push to pull everywhere, and read any accumulation of stock as a defect in the system.

Marshall Fisher · HBR · 1997

Functional vs innovative products

Stable, predictable products need a physically efficient chain; volatile, short-life products need a market-responsive one.

Told you toMatch the chain to the product. For most industrial goods that meant: minimise cost, and hold as little as possible.

Hau Lee · HBR · 2004

The Triple-A supply chain

Speed and cost are not enough. A chain must be agile, adaptable and aligned to survive.

Told you toBuild for change — sixteen years early. It was widely cited and thinly implemented, because none of the three had a number attached.

Corporate finance · throughout

The cash conversion cycle

CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO. Inventory is invested capital sitting in the denominator of return on capital.

Told you toCut days of inventory, because it is the fastest lever on free cash flow — and this is the one your bonus was actually tied to.

The unwritten assumption

Every model above treats uncertainty as something that happens on the demand side. Supply is a pipe: it has a length, and the length is a constant.

Read the formulas again and you can see it. Demand gets a distribution — a mean, a standard deviation, a service factor. Lead time gets a letter. Not a distribution: a letter, L, a fixed number of days you look up in a table. Suppliers were assumed available, ports were assumed open, and capacity was assumed purchasable at a price. This was not naive. For roughly thirty years, between the opening of global trade and 2020, it was empirically true nearly all of the time — which is precisely why nobody stress-tested it.

The five equations that ran the warehouse

Four of them are about physical goods. The fifth is the one that determined what anyone was actually paid to do.

The warnings that were already published

It is not true that nobody saw this coming. The resilience literature pre-dates COVID by fifteen years and it was accurate. It failed to change behaviour for a reason that is itself a measurement problem: the cost of resilience is certain, immediate and lands in one budget, while the benefit is probabilistic, deferred and lands in someone else's. There was no line on the P&L called "disruption that did not happen."

Chopra & Sodhi · MIT SMR · 2004
Separated recurrent risk (demand noise, which inventory handles) from disruptive risk (which it mostly does not), and warned that mitigation bought for one increases exposure to the other.
Christopher & Peck · 2004
Defined supply chain resilience as a design property — re-engineering, collaboration, agility, risk culture — rather than a stock level.
Yossi Sheffi · The Resilient Enterprise · 2005
Gave the disruption profile its shape: detection, first response, full impact, recovery. Argued flexibility beats redundancy because you use it every day rather than only in a crisis.
Hendricks & Singhal · 2003–05
Showed disruptions are not transient accounting events: announcing one was associated with large, persistent declines in shareholder value and operating performance that did not recover quickly.
02What actually broke2020 – 2022

Not a shortage. A failure of the assumption underneath the shortage

It is tempting to summarise 2020–22 as "there was not enough stuff." That is not what the data shows, and it is not the lesson. What broke was the premise that supply is a constant — and it broke in a correlated way, everywhere at once, which is the one failure mode none of the models could represent.

The sequence

Feb – Apr 2020
Supply stops, then demand inverts
Chinese production halts. Weeks later, spending rotates violently out of services and into goods. Two shocks with opposite signs arrive inside one quarter.

Peak pressure

Tariff changes and re-sourcing push the index back to +1.84σ in April 2026 — not a 2021-scale event, but a second live test of whether the capabilities built after 2020 were kept.

Four places the models were wrong — and one place the incentives were

Each of these maps directly onto a metric that exists today and did not exist, or was not tracked, in 2019.

01 · Uncertainty was on the wrong side
Demand got a distribution; supply got a constant. Every buffer in the network was sized for the variance that mattered least in 2021.
02 · Failures were assumed independent
Risk pooling, portfolio diversification and dual sourcing all rest on independence. Two suppliers on the same island, feeding through the same port, buying from the same tier three, are one supplier wearing two names.
03 · The process was assumed stationary
Forecasts and safety stocks are refit on history. When the data-generating process changes, history stops being evidence — and every model retrained on 2020–21 then carried the shock forward into 2023 as if it were signal.
04 · The map stopped at tier one
You cannot buffer, dual-source or insure a node you cannot name. Most firms discovered their critical dependency only when it stopped.
05 · And the scorecard was paying for all of it
This is the part that is not a modelling error. Purchase price variance rewarded the buyer who consolidated volume onto the cheapest single source. Asset utilisation rewarded the plant with no slack. Inventory turns rewarded the planner who cut the buffer, and reported nothing when the stockout arrived. Cash-to-cash improved when you pushed inventory onto a supplier who financed it worse than you did. Every one of these metrics was going the right way in 2019. The fragility was not a failure to hit the targets. It was the targets.
The formula, written out in full — the version the textbooks always had SS = z · √( L · σD² + D̄² · σL² ) ↑ ↑ demand variance lead-time variance — set to zero by assumption With σL = 0 this collapses to the familiar SS = z · σD · √L. Note the asymmetry: demand variance is multiplied by L, but lead-time variance is multiplied by the SQUARE of average demand. On a high-volume part, a few weeks of lead-time uncertainty outweighs everything the demand term can contribute.
03The correction2022 – 2026

The answer was not "hold more." It was "hold differently"

The popular account is that just-in-time died and just-in-case replaced it. The measured data says something sharper. Two sectors that entered 2019 holding almost exactly the same inventory relative to sales have ended up in opposite places — and the split tells you precisely which parts of the new doctrine were real.

Where the buffer stayed

Manufacturers hold more than they did before COVID, and have done consistently since 2021. This is the sector with single-sourced, long-lead, low-value, line-stopping components — the $3 connector that idles a $40,000 vehicle. For that class of part the economics of a buffer were always overwhelming and simply were not being calculated, because the aggregate metric a plant was judged on could not see the difference between a $3 connector and a pallet of finished goods.

Where it did not

Retail is now leaner than 2019 — 1.25 against a 1.46 average. It went from 1.09 and empty shelves, through a glut that produced billions in markdowns, to a position tighter than the one it started from. Nothing about retail's exposure profile rewards a permanent buffer: the products are substitutable, the lead times are shorter, and holding the wrong seasonal goods is more expensive than running out of them.

This is the actual finding. "Just-in-case replaced just-in-time" is false at the aggregate level — total US business inventories-to-sales is 0.11 below its 2019 average. The doctrine that replaced lean was not "hold more." It was hold according to consequence: heavy buffers on a small number of parts that can stop a line, thinner buffers than ever on everything else. That is a change in the composition of inventory, which is exactly why it needed a new set of metrics — the old ones only measured the level.
04The new doctrine2022 –

Nine things that replaced "minimise inventory"

None of these ideas are new. Almost all of them existed in the literature before 2019. What changed is that they acquired numbers, owners and budget — which is the difference between a framework and a practice.

Replaces ABC-by-spend

Segment by consequence

ABC classification ranked parts by annual purchase value, so the cheap ones got the least attention.

NowRank by what happens if it stops: sole-sourced, long re-qualification, no substitute, line-stopping. A class-C part by spend is routinely a class-A part by consequence.

Ptak & Smith · DDMRP

Position the buffer, don't just size it

A single end-to-end forecast transmits every error along the whole chain.

NowInsert decoupling points and hold buffers there, so variability is absorbed at chosen places instead of propagating. The question moved from "how much" to "where".

Simchi-Levi · HBR 2014

Measure impact, not probability

You cannot estimate the annual probability of a fire at a specific plant, and pretending otherwise produced risk registers full of invented numbers.

NowAssume the node is down. Measure time-to-recover and time-to-survive, and price the gap in revenue. It is auditable, and it is arguable in front of a CFO.

Resilinc · Interos · Everstream

n-tier visibility as a coverage number

Dual-sourcing tier one is worthless if both tier ones buy from the same tier three.

NowPercentage of critical BOM mapped to tier n becomes a tracked coverage KPI with a target, not a one-off consulting exercise.

Clark–Scarf · Graves & Willems

Multi-echelon optimisation

Setting safety stock site by site guarantees the network holds more in total than it needs, in the wrong places.

NowOptimise across echelons at once. The theory dates to 1960; commodity compute and clean data are what made it default practice after 2021.

M5 competition · 2020

Distributions, not point forecasts

A single expected-demand number plus a MAPE target tells you nothing about the tail you actually need to buffer.

NowForecast quantiles directly and score them with pinball loss. The inventory decision needs the 95th percentile, so forecast the 95th percentile.

Network design

China+1, regional, nearer

Two decades of single-country concentration turned a cost optimum into a correlated exposure.

NowQualified alternates in a second geography, regional service for regional demand. Genuinely under way — and, as §07 shows, considerably slower than the survey answers suggest.

Commercial terms

Buy capacity, not only goods

Inventory is one way to buy the right to supply later. It is not the only way, and often not the cheapest.

NowCapacity reservation, take-or-pay, multi-year supply agreements and pre-qualified second sources — an option premium paid in cash instead of in warehouse space.

Industrial policy

The state became a supply chain actor

For thirty years, sourcing was a commercial decision. It is now partly a regulated one.

NowSubsidy, export control, forced-labour import bans and tariffs change the landed cost and the legality of a source. Sourcing decisions carry a policy risk line that did not exist in the 2019 model.

The steel-man for just-in-time. It is worth saying plainly that JIT did not cause this, and the better analysts never claimed it did. Small batch sizes and short cycle times did not create the semiconductor shortage; a cancelled order book, a two-decade concentration of capacity and zero visibility below tier one did. Toyota — the originator — weathered the chip shortage better than most of its competitors precisely because it had a risk-part database and multi-month buffers on specific critical components, a policy it adopted after the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake. That is not an abandonment of TPS. It is TPS applied to a risk that had been made visible. The lesson is not "lean was wrong." It is "lean was being measured with instruments that could not see supply-side risk."
05The KPI ledger16 metric pairs

Every KPI that changed, and the exact reason it had to

This is the argument of the whole document, repeated sixteen times. Read each row left to right: the metric that governed the decision before 2019, what it was optimising for, the failure mode 2020–22 exposed in it, and the metric that now sits beside or in place of it. Almost none of the old metrics were wrong. They were incomplete in one specific, identifiable way — and the new metric is the patch for that specific hole.

Inventory01 / 16
Before 2019
Inventory turns
COGS ÷ average inventory
Optimised for: Capital efficiency, as one number covering the whole book. Higher was always better, and the board could read it without context.
After 2022
Inventory composition & health
Cycle · safety · strategic · excess & obsolete, each with its own target
Why it follows: Once you buffer by consequence rather than uniformly, the aggregate must move in confusing directions. The useful question stopped being how much inventory there is and became which inventory grew, and whether it is the inventory you chose.
Exposed
A high turn rate and a stockout look identical in the metric. It also prices a $3 line-stopping connector and a pallet of finished goods identically, because it can only see dollars — so the cheapest cut was always the most dangerous one.
Inventory02 / 16
Before 2019
Days inventory outstanding
365 × average inventory ÷ COGS — one target for the enterprise
Optimised for: A single, comparable, benchmarkable number that finance could set as a target and cascade down.
After 2022
Critical-component days of supply
Tracked separately per risk-designated part, with a floor, exempt from the aggregate target
Why it follows: You cannot let one target govern both a commodity fastener and a custom ASIC with a 52-week lead time. Carving critical parts out of the aggregate is what makes a deliberate buffer survive the next cost programme.
Exposed
A company-wide DIO target is a blunt instrument applied to a portfolio with hugely varying consequence. It was the mechanism by which planners were instructed to cut exactly the cheap, long-lead, sole-sourced parts that later stopped the line.
Sourcing03 / 16
Before 2019
Supplier count reduction
Number of active suppliers, tracked downward
Optimised for: Leverage, administrative cost, quality consistency. Consolidation was a stated strategic goal with a number attached.
After 2022
Single-source exposure & concentration
% of spend and % of critical BOM on one source; supplier concentration index
Why it follows: The identical underlying fact — few suppliers — now carries the sign it always deserved. What was an achievement metric became an exposure metric.
Exposed
Supplier rationalisation and supply concentration are the same act, reported with opposite signs. Firms hit their consolidation targets and reported it as progress right up until the single remaining source went offline.
Sourcing04 / 16
Before 2019
Purchase price variance
Actual purchase price − standard cost
Optimised for: Unit cost, measured per buyer, per quarter. The single most powerful individual incentive in procurement.
After 2022
Total cost of ownership incl. risk
Landed cost + qualification + carrying + expected disruption cost
Why it follows: If the second source's cost is charged to the buyer and its benefit is charged to nobody, no buyer will ever qualify one. Making the disruption term explicit is the only way the trade-off gets made at the level where it is decided.
Exposed
PPV rewards awarding volume to the cheapest single source and actively penalises paying to qualify a second one. The cost of resilience lands in this metric; the benefit never does. This is the incentive that built the fragility.
Sourcing05 / 16
Before 2019
Tier-1 audit & scorecard coverage
% of direct suppliers audited and scored
Optimised for: Quality, delivery and compliance assurance across the suppliers you actually contract with.
After 2022
n-tier visibility coverage
% of critical BOM mapped to tier 2 / 3, with site-level geolocation
Why it follows: Correlated failure lives below tier one. A map that stops at your contractual boundary cannot show you the shared node that makes your two suppliers one supplier.
Exposed
Dual-sourcing tier one is worthless if both tier ones buy from the same tier two, who buys from the same tier three. Firms with perfect tier-1 coverage discovered their true dependency only when it stopped.
Planning06 / 16
Before 2019
Forecast accuracy — MAPE
mean(|actual − forecast| ÷ actual)
Optimised for: A single-number report card on the demand plan, comparable across periods and planners.
After 2022
Quantile forecasts & pinball loss
Forecast the distribution; score the quantile you actually stock to (CRPS / pinball)
Why it follows: Safety stock has always been a quantile — the z in the formula is a service level. Forecasting the mean and then bolting a normal assumption onto it was always a workaround. Forecast the quantile you need.
Exposed
MAPE scores a point forecast, but an inventory decision is a statement about a tail. It also penalises volatile, high-consequence items hardest, so the planner's rational move is to focus on the SKUs that matter least.
Planning07 / 16
Before 2019
Schedule attainment
% of the production plan executed as planned
Optimised for: Stability and discipline on the shop floor: build what was scheduled.
After 2022
Plan stability & time-to-detect
Re-plan frequency and nervousness; latency from demand or supply signal to plan response
Why it follows: When the environment moves faster than the planning cycle, the speed and quality of re-planning matters more than adherence to the original plan.
Exposed
In a volatile regime, a plan produced on Monday is wrong by Wednesday. Attainment measures fidelity to a stale instruction, and rewards a planner who refuses to re-plan.
Service08 / 16
Before 2019
Fill rate / OTIF
Units or lines shipped complete and on time ÷ demanded, in aggregate
Optimised for: Customer service, measured as one enterprise-level percentage.
After 2022
Segmented, revenue-weighted service
Service level by customer and product criticality; margin- or revenue-weighted OTIF
Why it follows: During an allocation event the only useful question is which demand you protect. A metric that cannot distinguish between customers cannot inform that decision.
Exposed
A 98% aggregate fill rate can hide that the missing 2% was 40% of gross margin, or the one customer with a contractual penalty. Aggregation destroys precisely the information you need in a shortage.
Service09 / 16
Before 2019
Asset / capacity utilisation
Output ÷ theoretical capacity
Optimised for: Sweating capital. Every idle hour of a machine reads as waste.
After 2022
Surge capacity & time-to-scale
Sustainable headroom above plan; time to reach a defined uplift
Why it follows: Slack stopped being a defect and became an asset with a price. If you want to recover from a shock, someone has to have somewhere to recover into.
Exposed
Queueing theory has always said that as utilisation approaches 1, queue length and lead time go to infinity. A plant run at 100% has no ability to catch up after any disruption at all — it is the definition of no recovery capacity.
Network10 / 16
Before 2019
Landed cost per unit
Ex-works price + freight + duty + handling
Optimised for: The lowest delivered cost per unit, which for two decades meant the longest supply line to the lowest-wage geography.
After 2022
Cost-to-serve under scenarios
Landed cost evaluated across defined disruption and policy scenarios, not just base case
Why it follows: The comparison that matters is not which source is cheapest today but which is cheapest across the range of futures you are actually exposed to.
Exposed
Landed cost is a point estimate on a stable route. It has no term for the route closing, the port queueing, the freight rate going up twelvefold, or the tariff being changed by legislation.
Network11 / 16
Before 2019
DC count & pooling savings
Safety stock reduction from consolidation, ≈ √n
Optimised for: Risk pooling. Eppen's square-root law is correct and the savings are real.
After 2022
Geographic & chokepoint concentration
% of critical volume through one country, one port, one strait, one plant
Why it follows: Pooling and single-point-of-failure are the same structure. The saving is real and so is the exposure; both now have a number so the trade is made deliberately.
Exposed
Pooling assumes independent demand and an always-available supply line into the pooled node. Consolidation converts many partial failures into one total failure, and 2020–22 was a decade's worth of correlated events.
Finance12 / 16
Before 2019
Cash conversion cycle
CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO
Optimised for: Free cash flow, the fastest lever available to a CFO and the one most visible to investors.
After 2022
Network working capital & supplier health
Chain-level working capital cost; supplier financial-distress scoring
Why it follows: A metric you can improve by moving a cost onto someone whose failure would stop your line is not measuring what it claims to measure.
Exposed
CCC improves when you extend payment terms — which finances your inventory on a small supplier's balance sheet at their cost of capital, not yours. The network's total financing cost rises while your metric improves. Then the supplier fails and it is your disruption.
Finance13 / 16
Before 2019
— not measured —
Disruption did not appear anywhere in financial reporting
Optimised for: Nothing. There was no line on the P&L called "the disruption that did not happen," which is exactly why resilience never won a budget argument.
After 2022
Supply chain value-at-risk
Revenue-at-risk per node = exposure × (TTR − TTS), aggregated across the network
Why it follows: This is the change that made all the others fundable. Once exposure is denominated in revenue, a buffer stops being an operational preference and becomes a capital allocation decision on the same terms as any other.
Exposed
Firms could not compare the certain, immediate, budget-line cost of a second source against the uncertain, deferred benefit — so the comparison was never made and the default always won.
Risk14 / 16
Before 2019
Likelihood × impact heat map
Probability score 1–5 × impact score 1–5, on a risk register
Optimised for: An auditable-looking register that satisfied governance requirements.
After 2022
Time-to-recover / time-to-survive
TTR: days to restore a node. TTS: days you can serve without it. Exposure = the gap.
Why it follows: It deletes the unknowable input. You stop guessing how likely a failure is and instead measure two quantities you can actually establish, then price the difference. Auditable, comparable, and arguable in front of a CFO.
Exposed
Nobody can estimate the annual probability of a fire at a named plant, so the probability column was invented. Worse, genuine low-probability / catastrophic-impact events score mid-range on a 5×5 grid and receive no budget — the arithmetic actively hides the risks that matter.
Risk15 / 16
Before 2019
Business continuity plan exists
A document, reviewed annually, filed
Optimised for: Compliance. The plan's existence was the metric.
After 2022
Recovery time objective, rehearsed
Measured RTO per critical node; stress-test and playbook rehearsal cadence
Why it follows: Stress testing moved across from banking wholesale. An untested recovery time is an assumption, and this decade's lesson is that untested assumptions are where the exposure lives.
Exposed
Plans that had never been exercised failed on contact. Nobody knew how long it actually took to re-qualify an alternate part, because nobody had ever done it under time pressure.
Governance16 / 16
Before 2019
Supplier code of conduct signed
% of suppliers who have signed the code
Optimised for: Documented compliance at the tier-1 contractual boundary.
After 2022
Traceability to source
% of restricted or high-risk inputs traced to origin; Scope 3 coverage
Why it follows: The same n-tier map that supports resilience is now also a legal and regulatory requirement. Two separate arguments now fund the same capability, which is a large part of why this one stuck.
Exposed
Forced-labour import bans, origin rules and emissions accounting all attach to the source, not to your direct supplier. A signature from tier one says nothing about tier three, and unmappable inputs became seizable at the border.
The pattern across all sixteen. Every old metric is an aggregate, a point estimate, or a cost. Every new one is a decomposition, a distribution, or an exposure. That is the whole shift in one line: the pre-2019 system measured the average of a stable world in a single number, and the post-2022 system measures the shape of an unstable one — because the average is exactly what tells you nothing during a shock.
06Risk assessmentThe conceptual shifts

Seven changes underneath all of it

Strip away the individual metrics and the same seven moves sit underneath every one of them. These are the ones worth arguing about, because they determine whether a firm has genuinely changed its model of the world or has simply bought some software.

01Uncertainty is on the demand side→Uncertainty is on both sides

The single most consequential change. Every classical model gives demand a distribution and supply a constant. The post-2020 model gives lead time, yield, capacity and availability their own distributions — which means the buffer calculation, the forecast and the network design all have to be redone, not re-parameterised.

02Risk = probability × impact→Risk = impact, failure assumed

The probability of a specific plant failing in a specific year is not knowable, and every register that claimed otherwise was recording a guess dressed as an estimate. Assuming failure and measuring recovery time removes the fabricated input entirely. It is a weaker claim about the world and a much stronger basis for a decision.

03Failures are independent→Failures are correlated

Risk pooling, portfolio diversification and dual sourcing all rest on independence — and independence is exactly what a shared tier-three supplier, a shared port, a shared country or a shared cloud region destroys. Most of what looks like new technology in this field, from n-tier mapping to network digital twins, exists to find correlation that the org chart hides.

04Inventory is a level→Inventory is a portfolio

A single number to be minimised became a set of positions with different jobs: cycle stock buys efficiency, safety stock buys service, strategic stock buys time, and excess and obsolete stock is a loss you have not booked yet. They are managed by different people against different targets, and aggregating them destroyed the only information that mattered.

05Forecast the expected value→Forecast the distribution

An inventory decision has always been a quantile decision — the service factor z in the safety-stock formula is a quantile. Pairing a point forecast with an assumed normal distribution was a computational convenience from an era without compute. Forecasting the quantile directly, and scoring it as a quantile, closes a gap that was always there.

06Minimise local cost→Maximise network survivability

The objective function itself changed. It was: minimise cost subject to a service level. It is now: minimise total cost including the expected cost of disruption, subject to surviving a defined set of node failures. The constraint is new — and a new constraint is a genuinely different problem, not a different weighting of the same one.

07Risk lives in a register→Risk lives on the balance sheet

The change that made the other six fundable. As long as exposure was a colour on a heat map, it lost every budget argument to a cost saving denominated in currency. Expressing it as revenue-at-risk put it on the same terms as everything else a capital committee compares — which is the only reason any of this outlived the crisis that prompted it.

Set the two panels beside each other and the gap is stark. In 2023, 78% of executives reported increasing inventory buffers. But it means survey answers about resilience cannot be read as evidence that balance sheets changed.

The honest verdict

Retreated · intent peaked in 2022
Dual sourcing fell from 81% to 73% and regionalisation from 64% to 60% between 2022 and 2024. McKinsey's own reading is that momentum has plateaued. The 2023 cost-cutting cycle arrived before most resilience programmes had finished paying for themselves.
Retreated · the 2022 glut was not precision
Target at +43% and Walmart at +33% year on year were not firms executing a considered buffer strategy. They were firms that ordered aggressively into a demand signal that had already turned — the bullwhip again, in the opposite direction, eighteen months later.
Retreated · resilience often meant software
Visibility platforms and control towers are easier to buy than second sources are to qualify, and they book as one project rather than a permanent cost increase. A dashboard showing an exposure you have not funded a response to is not resilience.
Stuck · physical and legal facts
Fabs and plants that were built are still built. Industrial policy, export controls and origin rules are still law. Long-term capacity agreements still run. Manufacturing still holds more inventory relative to sales than it did in 2019, five years on.
Stuck · the measurement system
This is the durable part, and it is why the KPI ledger is the centre of this document. Metrics outlive the enthusiasm that created them, because they are embedded in systems, reports and comp plans. A firm that added revenue-at-risk to its planning review in 2021 is still running it.
The live test, now
Pressure is back at +1.84σ as of April 2026 on tariff and re-sourcing effects. This is the first shock since 2020 to hit firms that claim to have built the capability. Whether TTR and exposure figures were maintained — or quietly stopped being produced in 2024 — is about to be visible in who absorbs it.

Where this document is weak. Four things a careful reader should hold against it. One: sector-level inventory ratios average over enormous firm-level variation — a sector can be flat while half its firms doubled their buffers and the other half halved them. Two: the survey waves are not a matched panel, so the year-on-year movements in §07 are indicative rather than measured change. Three: the KPI ledger describes practice at large, well-resourced firms; the median manufacturer has adopted far less of it than the literature implies. Four: several framework attributions are conventional rather than uncontested — priority disputes exist for the EOQ formula and for parts of the bullwhip literature, and the dates given are for the canonical publication, not the first idea.
End of document

If you take one thing from this: the pre-2019 system was not badly run. It was well run against an incomplete scorecard, and the scorecard's blind spot was supply-side uncertainty. Everything in §05 is an attempt to fix that one hole. Whether it holds depends less on what firms believe than on whether the numbers are still being produced in three years — which is the only reliable test of whether a doctrine actually changed.
