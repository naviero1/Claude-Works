The Thirty
Thirty named dishes across thirty restaurants, ranked on seven health criteria
plus flavor and cost. Each entry is one specific dish whose figures describe
exactly that dish, followed by 116 alternative orders that each carry their own
score. Every restaurant was verified to have a live DoorDash storefront; its store
ID is printed with the dish.

How the recommended dish is chosen
Each entry leads with one dish. That dish is the highest-scoring option at the restaurant that also works as a
complete meal — roughly 380 calories and 30 g of protein or more, and orderable from the standard menu.
Alternatives are scored on the identical model, so where one beats the recommendation it is marked ▲ and
the reason the recommendation stands anyway is stated. Nothing is hidden behind a judgement call that is
not written down.

The Nine Criteria
Seven health criteria carry equal weight; sugar carries 0.7, and flavor and cost carry 0.5 each. Neither flavor nor
cost can rescue an unhealthy dish — at half weight they reorder the middle of the list without overturning it.
Kidney ×1.0
Sodium alone. Full marks at 350 mg, zero at
2,000.
Liver ×1.0
Saturated fat 65%, calorie load 35%.
Muscle ×1.0
Protein per 100 kcal 70%, absolute protein
30%.
Gut ×1.0
Live ferments 60%, plant-type count 40%.
Vinegar pickles score zero.
Energy ×1.0
Refined-carb load, fiber, protein presence —
a glycemic proxy.
Inflammation ×1.0
Omega-3 60%, plant and herb diversity
40%.
Sugar ×0.7
Total sugars. Full marks at 3 g, zero at 22 g.
Flavor ×0.5
Char, acid, ferment, aromatics, surviving
richness and texture — minus a penalty for
flavor the health edit removes.
Cost ×0.5
DoorDash-listed price at 60%, price per
gram of protein at 40%. Rewards both a
cheap meal and good protein value.
How Flavor Is Scored
Not a taste opinion and not a star rating. Flavor here measures where a dish's intensity comes from, and how
much of it survives being ordered the healthy way. Six positive components and one penalty, scaled to 0–10.
Char 0–3
Grill, charcoal, tandoor,
rotisserie, wok, hard sear.
Maillard flavor is free — it costs
no sodium and no fat, and no
health edit removes it.
Acid 0–2
Lime, vinegar, tamarind, sumac.
The single most underrated
substitute for salt.
Ferment 0–2
Fermented flavor — kimchi,
doenjang, fish sauce, miso,
yogurt. Scored separately from
the gut criterion, which cares
whether cultures are alive.
Aromatics 0–3
Herbs, whole spice, alliums,
chili, ginger, lemongrass.
Weighted highest because it is
the largest reservoir of flavor
that carries no nutritional cost
at all.
Richness 0–2
Fat that survives the healthy
build: avocado, tahini, olive oil,
oily fish. Cheese and
mayonnaise usually do not
survive and so do not count.
Texture 0–2
Contrast. A bowl of uniformly
soft things scores low however
well seasoned it is.
Strip penalty −0–3
How much flavor the health edit
actually removes. Declining a
vinaigrette, skipping cheese,
choosing greens over rice. This
is what separates a Chipotle
salad from a plate of larb.
The pattern —
Cuisines built on char, acid and
aromatics keep their flavor
when you strip salt and fat.
Cuisines built on sauce, cheese
and frying do not. That is why
Thai, Vietnamese, Korean,
Turkish and Ethiopian dishes
cluster at the top of the flavor
column and American bowl
chains cluster at the bottom.
The Thirty, Ranked
Bars show where each dish earns its score — tall is good, short is the weak point. The seven dark bars are health;
the two separated bars are flavor (amber) and cost (slate). Beneath each dish sit the alternative orders at that
restaurant, each scored on the same model, so the trade-off in swapping a protein or a base is a number rather
than an assertion.
