Chapter one

What money does and does not buy

Across all 60 dishes, the correlation between what a dish costs on DoorDash and how healthy it scores is +0.05. Squared, that is well under one percent of the variation: price tells you essentially nothing about whether a meal is good for you. Leave out any single dish and it never leaves the range -0.03 to +0.14, so it is not one outlier holding it down.

Money does buy something. The correlation between price and flavor is +0.41 — modest, but five times the size and in a direction you can feel. Expensive food in this set is more interesting to eat and no better for you. Everything in this chapter follows from those two numbers.

Chapter two

The counterintuitive part

Only 3 of the 10 swaps above raise the dish's score while cutting sodium. Look at what the rest give up: Namu's banchan plates save 600 mg and lose 26 g of protein. Southern Spice's idli-without-sambar saves 560 mg and drops the score by half a point. The reliable way to cut sodium at a restaurant is to eat less of the meal, which is a real option and a poor one. The exceptions — the ones where you cut salt without cutting food — are almost all sauce refusals, and they are listed above under “where the salt actually is.”

Chapter three

What flavor actually costs

The first edition argued that cuisines built on char, acid and aromatics keep their flavor when you strip out salt and fat, while cuisines built on sauce, cheese and frying do not. The first half of that is right and the second half is aimed at the wrong nutrient.

Take the 22 dishes scoring 7.5 or better on flavor and the 14 scoring 5.5 or worse. The flavorful group carries +572 mg more sodium, at t = 4.5. It also carries +2.0 g more saturated fat, at t = 2.4, which is on the edge of meaning nothing at all.

The sodium gap is not one dish doing the work. Leave any single dish out and it stays between 520 and 655 mg, never falling below t = 4.1; on medians rather than means the gap is +650 mg.

So flavor in this set is bought mainly with salt, not with fat, and the first edition was policing the wrong nutrient. That is also why there is no health-flavor tradeoff between individual dishes — the correlation is −0.12, nil. The tradeoff is not a property of a dish. It is a property of a kitchen's whole seasoning strategy, and it shows up only when you group by cuisine.

Read the last two columns together

Salt is what flavor costs. It is not the only way a cuisine loses the health column. Korean, Sichuan and Vietnamese kitchens sit high on flavor and low on health entirely on sodium — their saturated fat is unremarkable. Caribbean and Mexican kitchens lose it a different way, on 11.0 g and 10.7 g of saturated fat against a set median near 5.0, and they do not get leading flavor scores in exchange. Those are the two failure modes, and only one of them buys you anything.

Why the set-level correlation is nearly zero

It is not that health and flavor are unrelated. It is that two criteria pull in opposite directions and very nearly cancel. Decompose the covariance and kidney contributes −0.31 while gut contributes +0.28 — the salty, fermented kitchens lose on one exactly as fast as they win on the other. Take the kidney criterion out and health correlates +0.12 with flavor; take gut out instead and it goes to −0.31. The flat headline number is an artifact of the model's own weighting, not a fact about food.

The reason those two criteria fight is physical rather than statistical: almost every live ferment a restaurant can sell you is preserved in salt. Ask this list for a dish scoring 7 or better on both gut and kidney and it returns 0 of 60. Only Goorsha clears 6 on both, at gut 6.2 and kidney 7.0. Yogurt is the one escape route in this data — labneh, cacık and raita carry live cultures without a brine — which is why the Levantine and Turkish entries come closest.

The market rate for a flavor point

Regress flavor on sodium across all 60 dishes and the exchange rate is 436 mg per point (r = +0.60). That is the going price of taste on this list. 11 dishes beat it by more than a full point — and 9 of those 11 are grilled. This is the first edition's thesis, stated as a number rather than an assertion: char, acid and aromatics are how a kitchen buys flavor without paying in salt.

The sample

What a ranking leaves out

A ranking is only as good as the set it ranks, and the first edition's was assembled from a narrow slice of what actually delivers here. 18 of its 60 entries — 30% — were build-your-own bowls or poke, and 4 of its top ten. That is not a finding about food. It is a finding about how a list gets built: assembled-to-order formats are easy to score because you control every component, so they get picked, and they then win on criteria the format is designed to satisfy.

The omissions were more telling than the concentrations. A document written about food in North Carolina contained no barbecue. The next section adds 27 entries against that list, and the categories it still cannot fill are named there.

Categories that really are empty here

Searched, and not padded to fill a row. Somali, Eritrean and Sudanese kitchens do not exist as standalone restaurants in the Triangle - the searches return only Ethiopian houses, one of which markets itself as Ethiopian and Eritrean but cooks an injera-and-wot menu indistinguishable from the two already scored. The same is true of Ghanaian, Georgian, Armenian and Israeli cooking: no restaurant in the named towns, and the nearest Georgian option sits outside the area entirely. Taiwanese kitchens do exist here, but none of them carries a dish that clears the bar this document sets for a complete meal.

How the new entries score against the same measures

They are worse food, on this model, and predictably so: mean health 5.43 against 5.89 for the original sixty, on +233 mg more sodium at almost exactly the same price ($19.15 against $18.82). That is what filling the gaps costs: 17 of the 27 are grilled, smoked or fried, and the compendium’s whole argument is that those formats buy their flavor with salt. Mean flavor is 5.30 against 6.69 — lower, because the group also contains the steamed and diet-menu orders nobody chooses.

The exceptions are the point. Talullas’s balık şiş would rank 3rd of 87, and Alpaca Peruvian Charcoal Chicken clears health 6.0 at $11.29 — cheaper than anything in the original set that does. Within these 27 the price–health correlation is +0.20, which is higher than the +0.05 across the original sixty and still small enough to be worth nothing.

Why each one is here

Turkish

Talullas — Balık Şiş

The second Turkish entry earns its place by not repeating the first: no bulgur, no pide, no rice — a char-grilled wild salmon şiş that arrives on arugula and nothing else. It is the only plate in this sweep with no starch on it at all. The haydari follows Turkish mezze logic and does the same job the compendium's cacık does at Bosphorus.

Charcoal on oily fish is the best argument in Turkish grilling: skin blisters, the fat renders into the char. Arugula is peppery and bitter, lemon is aggressive, the strained yogurt is thick and garlicky. Almost nothing is declined except the bread basket. The gap is fibre and vegetables — this plate is fish, leaves and yogurt.

Order it: Balık Şiş · char-grilled wild salmon with arugula salad · add Haydari ($8, cream of yogurt with dill and garlic) · REFUSE the bread · lemon over all of it

Peruvian

Alpaca Peruvian Charcoal Chicken — ¼ Chicken w/ White Combo

The sides are the whole story: two are included, and black beans and house salad sit in the same price tier as fries and white rice. Take them and a quarter of charcoal white meat delivers 53 grams of protein for $11.29 — 21 cents a gram, among the three cheapest in the book. The soy-heavy brine is what keeps sodium near 1,300.

Real charcoal does real work: the skin browns properly, the ají panca and cumin in the overnight brine push through, and a low soy savour sits underneath. What you surrender by refusing the ají verde is most of the richness — it is mayonnaise with green chile, and it is worth roughly a point of flavour against a point of health.

Order it: ¼ Chicken w/ White Combo (breast & wing) · black beans · house salad, dressing on the side and mostly unused · REFUSE the hand-cut fries and the white rice · REFUSE the yellow sauce outright; one ounce of green sauce is a fair trade but costs about two tenths of a point

Cuban

Carmen's Cuban Cafe & Lounge — Ceviche Salad

Twenty years in Morrisville, and the lean order is not the ropa vieja — it is the ceviche, moved onto the house salad. Every entree here arrives with three sides, and that structure is what sinks the braises: rice, beans and maduros add four hundred calories before the meat does anything. The salad version skips all of it.

Citrus cure, raw onion, a little cilantro, and whatever the vinaigrette brings. It is the quietest dish in this batch — no char, no ferment, almost no fat — and the Cuban kitchen's actual weapons, mojo and sofrito and slow-roast pork, are all sitting on the plates you just declined. Bright, clean, faintly under-seasoned.

Order it: Ceviche Salad · vinaigrette on the side and about half of it used · REFUSE the Cuban bread · do NOT convert it into an entree plate — the three included sides are what ruin every other dish here · add black beans (+$3.95) only if you need the meal larger, and know it costs about 450 mg of sodium

The model

Nine criteria, written out

The first edition described its criteria in prose. This one prints them as functions, because a scoring model you cannot recompute is an opinion wearing a number's clothes.

Six of the nine are arithmetic on the six macros and the price, and are reproduced here exactly: feeding the first edition's own published macros back through them returns every one of its 60 printed overall scores to within 0.10, with a mean deviation of 0.023. Three — gut, energy and inflammation — are human judgements on a rubric, and no formula recovers them. Where a shape can be inferred from the pattern of scores it is given with an approximation sign; where it cannot, the criterion is simply marked as judged.

Composite: health = (KID + LIV + MUS + GUT + ENE + INF + 0.7×SUG) / 6.7, then overall = (health×6.7 + FLAVOR×0.5 + COST×0.5) / 7.7.

The first edition wrote that flavor and cost at half weight “reorder the middle of the list without overturning it.” That is checkable, and it holds. Multiply each column's weight by the range it actually spans across these 60 dishes and you get the most it could ever move an overall score: KID 1.26, GUT 1.22, INF 1.22, MUS 1.13, LIV 1.12, ENE 0.97, SUG 0.91, cost 0.58, flavor 0.42. Flavor and cost are the two smallest levers in the model by some distance.

Kidney

×1.0

Sodium alone.

KID = clamp( 10 × (2000 − sodium_mg) / 1650 )

Full marks at 350 mg, zero at 2,000. Linear between, which means a dish at 2,100 mg and one at 3,000 mg both score zero.

Liver

×1.0

Saturated fat 65%, calorie load 35%.

LIV = 0.65 × clamp( 10 × (16.5 − satfat_g) / 14.5 )
    + 0.35 × clamp( 10 × (1000 − cal) / 500 )

The only criterion whose anchors had to be fitted rather than read off. Reproduces the published scores with a mean deviation of 0.13.

Muscle

×1.0

Protein density 70%, absolute protein 30%.

MUS = 0.7 × clamp( 10 × (perKcal − 2) / 7 )
    + 0.3 × clamp( 10 × (protein_g − 20) / 25 )
  where perKcal = protein_g / (cal/100)

Rewards protein per calorie first, so a light dish with 30 g beats a heavy one with 40.

Gut

×1.0

Live ferments 60%, plant-type count 40%.

GUT ≈ 0.6 × (live-ferment ladder, 0–4)
    + 0.4 × (plant types, capped at 19)

A judgement, not a calculation — the ladder above is the smallest one consistent with all 60 published scores, not a formula the first edition states. Vinegar pickles score zero. Fiber carries no weight at all — see the audit below. Judged, not computed.

Energy

×1.0

Refined-carb load, fiber, protein presence.

ENE — rubric, 0–10

A glycemic proxy assembled by hand. It correlates with fiber at +0.38, which is the closest thing to a check available on it. Judged, not computed.

Inflammation

×1.0

Omega-3 60%, plant and herb diversity 40%.

INF ≈ 0.6 × (omega-3 ladder, 0–3)
    + 0.4 × (plant and herb diversity, 0–7)

Inferred, like the gut ladder, rather than recovered exactly. The omega-3 ladder does not distinguish plant ALA from marine EPA and DHA, which overstates flaxseed — see the audit. Judged, not computed.

Sugar

×0.7

Total sugars.

SUG = clamp( 10 × (22 − sugar_g) / 19 )

Full marks at 3 g, zero at 22. Uses total sugars, so fruit and plain dairy are penalised exactly like added sugar.

Flavor

×0.5

Six components, minus what the health edit removes.

FLAVOR = ( char 0–3 + acid 0–2
         + ferment 0–2 + aromatics 0–3
         + richness 0–2 + texture 0–2
         − strip penalty 0–3 ) / 14 × 10

Half-points allowed. Not a taste rating: it measures where a dish's intensity comes from and how much survives ordering it the healthy way. Judged, not computed.

Cost

×0.5

Listed price 60%, price per gram of protein 40%.

COST = 0.6 × clamp( 10 × (35 − price) / 25 )
     + 0.4 × clamp( 10 × (0.90 − perGram)
                    / 0.65 )
  where perGram = price / protein_g

Rewards a cheap meal and good protein value together. Ignores portion size, which is its main flaw.

Before you trust a number

7 of the 60 entries are built from a chain’s own published nutrition documents — 2 more than the first edition claimed, because Chopt and Sweetgreen both publish figures it never consulted. 2 are partial. The other 51 are reasoned from how the dish is put together, and that reasoning leaves fingerprints: 46 of them land on a round multiple of 50 mg of sodium and 42 on a multiple of 20 calories. Real nutrition panels do not do that. The published entries here read 380, 400, 415, 542, 575, 775 and 880 calories.

The gap in that table is not what it looks like

Read naively, published entries carry 482 mg less sodium than estimated ones and score 1.35 lower on flavor. That reads like an estimator who reaches for a high, dull number when they do not know one. It is not. Every one of the 9 entries with published or partial data is an assembled bowl or a plate of raw fish. Not one of the 36 dishes here that meets heat — grilled, stewed or steamed — rests on a published number. Nobody publishes nutrition for food cooked to order over fire, so the published tier and the bowl-chain tier are the same tier.

Control for that and the apparent bias mostly disappears. Compare only the assembled and raw dishes with each other — 9 verified against 15 estimated, same format — and the flavor gap collapses to +0.01 and the sodium gap actually reverses to -136 mg. The estimator was not systematically pessimistic. The sample was systematically narrow.

Which leaves the caveat in a more useful place. Trust the ranking’s treatment of bowls, salads and raw fish, because that is where the real numbers are. Treat every grilled, stewed and steamed entry as a construction estimate that no restaurant has confirmed — and note that this edition’s largest corrections, Sassool and El Cuscatleco, both came from exactly that group.

Against interest

Where this model misfires

Every criterion in this document was checked against current dietary guidance and the primary literature. Five produce results their own author would struggle to defend. They are printed here because a ranking that never argues with itself is not worth reading, and because a reader who knows where a model is weak can still use it.

The gut criterion gives fiber no weight at all

It scores live ferments at 60% and a count of plant types at 40%. Fiber contributes nothing, and across the set fiber and the gut score correlate at only +0.30. There is a narrow defence — a 17-week trial found a fermented-food diet raised microbiome diversity where a high-fiber diet did not — but that defence is about alpha diversity, not about gut health as a reader would understand the phrase, and the criterion is labelled for the reader. As built it is a live-culture detector wearing a gut-health label.

Wastyk et al., Cell, August 2021.

The kidney criterion stops measuring at 2,000 mg

It runs linearly from 350 mg to 2,000 and then floors, so every dish past that point scores zero and none can be told apart. This edition produced its own demonstration. Chosun Ok’s stew was corrected twice: first up to about 4,000 mg, once the 1 lb kimchi side was counted at USDA’s 498 mg per 100 g, and then back down to 1,700 mg when the kimchi side turned out not to exist on the menu at all. The first correction nearly doubled the dish’s sodium and the kidney criterion did not move at all — it was already at zero. Only the second correction, which brought the figure back under the ceiling, changed anything. A scale that cannot tell 2,100 mg from 4,000 is not measuring the thing it is named for at the end of its range that matters most.

Sugar is scored as total, not added

Current dietary guidance targets added and free sugars and explicitly exempts the sugars in fruit and plain milk. This criterion does not distinguish them, so a bowl of fruit and a honey vinaigrette are penalised identically. First Watch’s Tri-Fecta — eggs, avocado, seasonal fruit and whole-grain toast — takes a sugar score of 5.3 mostly on the fruit, where the same 12 g from a glaze would cost exactly as much.

Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2025–2030.

The omega-3 ladder treats flaxseed as if it were fish

The inflammation criterion scores omega-3 on a single ladder that does not distinguish plant ALA from marine EPA and DHA. It should. A review of 13 randomised trials found high-dose flaxseed and echium oil produced no increase in the omega-3 index at all, against reliable increases from fish oil; conversion of ALA to EPA runs at a few percent and to DHA below one. Every entry here scored for flaxseed is scored generously.

Lane et al., Critical Reviews in Food Science and Nutrition, 2022.

Cost knows nothing about how much food you get

It rewards a low price and good protein value and ignores portion size entirely. A 320-calorie dish and a 780-calorie dish at the same price and protein score identically, though only one of them is dinner. On a list whose portions range more than twofold, this is the criterion most likely to mislead.

And what held up

Two of the model’s more contestable calls survived the check. Weighting the liver criterion on saturated fat rather than sugar is right: in a head-to-head overfeeding trial, saturated fat raised liver fat substantially more than unsaturated fat or simple sugars did — though the same guidance also names excess fructose, so a small added-sugar term belongs in that criterion and is not there. And the rule that vinegar quick-pickles score zero is correct: quick-process pickles are brined briefly then covered in vinegar with no fermentation step at all, a real and frequently-missed distinction. Labneh, cacık and raita are confirmed live-culture items, conditional only on the yogurt not having been heat-treated after culturing — which no menu will tell you.

Method and provenance

What a score is

Each entry is one specific dish, ordered one specific way, and every figure describes that dish as built — not the restaurant, and not the menu item as it arrives by default. Where the build says to refuse something, the numbers assume you refused it.

Prices

DoorDash-listed. Published measurements put the delivery-app premium at roughly 10–20% above in-store, averaging about 15% — the first edition said 15–25%, which is high at both ends. Every cost score here is computed on the delivery price, so the cost column is systematically harsher than an in-store comparison would be.

Storefronts

57 of the 60 entries print a DoorDash store id; three print a brand-level business id or the word “chain”. Thirty-five restaurants were independently spot-checked for this edition and all thirty-five exist and appear to be trading, with 29 of the printed ids matched to the right restaurant and address. Distances are straight-line from Research Triangle Park, and an active storefront is not the same as delivery to a given address — radius is computed per address at checkout.

What this is not

Not medical advice, and not a clinical instrument. The criteria are a defensible way to compare restaurant meals with each other; they are not a diagnosis and the anchor points are judgement calls, some of which are argued with above.

Reproducibility

The six computed criteria are printed as functions and the dataset behind this edition is machine-readable. Any score here can be recomputed, disagreed with, and refitted with different anchors.
