 What changed in this edition
 
- The numbers were checked, and some were wrong. Every nutrition figure the first edition attributed to a published document was re-read against that document. 9 entries had to be rescored: Bul Box from 23rd to 57th; Sassool from 3rd to 32nd. Everything checked is listed in the verification log at the end, including what came back clean and what could not be settled.
- The front page is new. The first edition opened with four findings, three of which were trivia about a single restaurant each and the fourth about research method rather than food. They have been replaced with findings drawn from the whole set, led by what the data says about money.
- The two lists are now one. The first edition printed “The Thirty” and “The Next Thirty” as separate ranked lists, but the split tracked research depth rather than score: 27 entries in the second list outscored the weakest in the first. Everything is now ranked in one sequence.
- The scoring model is printed, not described. Every criterion that can be computed from the macros is given as an actual function, recovered by fitting the first edition's own published scores. Anyone can recompute anything here, including the parts they disagree with.
- The sample was too narrow. Eighteen of the original sixty entries were build-your-own bowls or poke — 30% of the set, and four of the top ten — in a state whose own barbecue did not appear at all. That gap is quantified below.
 

 
 Chapter one
 

## What money does and does not buy
 
 
Across all 60 dishes, the correlation between what a dish costs on DoorDash and how healthy it scores is +0.08. Squared, that is well under one percent of the variation: price tells you essentially nothing about whether a meal is good for you. Leave out any single dish and it never leaves the range 0.00 to +0.17, so it is not one outlier holding it down.
 
Money does buy something. The correlation between price and flavor is +0.39 — modest, but five times the size and in a direction you can feel. Expensive food in this set is more interesting to eat and no better for you. Everything in this chapter follows from those two numbers.

## Sodium is the currency
 
 
Sodium separates this list more than any other single number, and the useful way to hold it is not as a total but as a rate: how much sodium you are paying for each gram of protein you actually get. On that measure the spread across the set is more than twentyfold.
 
DICED delivers protein at 2.5 mg of sodium per gram. Chosun Ok charges 100.0 mg for the same gram. Both are on the same list, in the same city, at similar prices.

#### The counterintuitive part
 
Only 3 of the 12 swaps above raise the dish's score while cutting sodium. Look at what the rest give up: Namu's banchan plates save 600 mg and lose 26 g of protein. Southern Spice's idli-without-sambar saves 560 mg and drops the score by half a point. The reliable way to cut sodium at a restaurant is to eat less of the meal, which is a real option and a poor one. The exceptions — the ones where you cut salt without cutting food — are almost all sauce refusals, and they are listed above under “where the salt actually is.”
 

 
 Chapter three
 

## What flavor actually costs
 
 
The first edition argued that cuisines built on char, acid and aromatics keep their flavor when you strip out salt and fat, while cuisines built on sauce, cheese and frying do not. The first half of that is right and the second half is aimed at the wrong nutrient.
 
Take the 22 dishes scoring 7.5 or better on flavor and the 14 scoring 5.5 or worse. The flavorful group carries +667 mg more sodium, at t = 3.8 — a separation nothing about this sample can explain away. It also carries +1.7 g more saturated fat, at t = 2.0, which is on the edge of meaning nothing at all.
 
So flavor in this set is bought mainly with salt, not with fat, and the first edition was policing the wrong nutrient. That is also why there is no health-flavor tradeoff between individual dishes — the correlation is −0.05, nil. The tradeoff is not a property of a dish. It is a property of a kitchen's whole seasoning strategy, and it shows up only when you group by cuisine.

A ranking is only as good as the set it ranks, and this one was assembled from a narrow slice of what actually delivers here. 18 of the 60 entries — 30% — are build-your-own bowls or poke, and 4 of the top ten are. That is not a finding about food. It is a finding about how the list was built: assembled-to-order formats are easy to score because you control every component, so they get picked, and they then win on criteria the format is designed to satisfy.
 
The omissions are more telling than the concentrations. This is a document written about food in North Carolina that contains no barbecue.

 The model
 

## Nine criteria, written out
 
 
The first edition described its criteria in prose. This one prints them as functions, because a scoring model you cannot recompute is an opinion wearing a number's clothes.
 
Six of the nine are arithmetic on the six macros and the price, and are reproduced here exactly: feeding the published macros back through them returns every one of the 60 printed overall scores to within 0.10. Three — gut, energy and inflammation — are human judgements on a rubric, and no formula will recover them. Those three are marked.
 
Composite: health = (KID + LIV + MUS + GUT + ENE + INF + 0.7×SUG) / 6.7, then overall = (health×6.7 + FLAVOR×0.5 + COST×0.5) / 7.7. Flavor and cost at half weight cannot rescue an unhealthy dish; they reorder the middle of the list.
 
 
 

### Kidney×1.0
 
Sodium alone.
 
KID = clamp( 10 × (2000 − sodium_mg) / 1650 )
 
Full marks at 350 mg, zero at 2,000. Linear between, which means a dish at 2,100 mg and one at 3,000 mg both score zero.
 
 

### Liver×1.0
 
Saturated fat 65%, calorie load 35%.
 
LIV = 0.65 × clamp( 10 × (16.5 − satfat_g) / 14.5 )
 + 0.35 × clamp( 10 × (1000 − cal) / 500 )
 
Recovered by fitting the published scores; reproduces them to within 0.18 on average.
 
 

### Muscle×1.0
 
Protein density 70%, absolute protein 30%.
 
MUS = 0.7 × clamp( 10 × (perKcal − 2) / 7 )
 + 0.3 × clamp( 10 × (protein_g − 20) / 25 )
 where perKcal = protein_g / (cal/100)
 
Rewards protein per calorie first, so a light dish with 30 g beats a heavy one with 40.
 
 

### Gut×1.0
 
Live ferments 60%, plant-type count 40%.
 
GUT = 0.6 × (live-ferment ladder, 0–4)
 + 0.4 × (plant types, capped at 19)
 
A judgement, not a calculation. Vinegar pickles score zero. Fiber carries no weight at all — see the audit below. Judged, not computed.
 
 

### Energy×1.0
 
Refined-carb load, fiber, protein presence.
 
ENE — rubric, 0–10
 
A glycemic proxy assembled by hand. It correlates with fiber at +0.44, which is the closest thing to a check available on it. Judged, not computed.
 
 

### Inflammation×1.0
 
Omega-3 60%, plant and herb diversity 40%.
 
INF = 0.6 × (omega-3 ladder, 0–3)
 + 0.4 × (plant and herb diversity, 0–7)
 
The omega-3 ladder does not distinguish plant ALA from marine EPA and DHA, which overstates flaxseed. Judged, not computed.
 
 

### Sugar×0.7
 
Total sugars.
 
SUG = clamp( 10 × (22 − sugar_g) / 19 )
 
Full marks at 3 g, zero at 22. Uses total sugars, so fruit and plain dairy are penalised exactly like added sugar.
 
 

### Flavor×0.5
 
Six components, minus what the health edit removes.
 
FLAVOR = ( char 0–3 + acid 0–2
 + ferment 0–2 + aromatics 0–3
 + richness 0–2 + texture 0–2
 − strip penalty 0–3 ) / 14 × 10
 
Half-points allowed. Not a taste rating: it measures where a dish's intensity comes from and how much survives ordering it the healthy way. Judged, not computed.
 
 

### Cost×0.5
 
Listed price 60%, price per gram of protein 40%.
 
COST = 0.6 × clamp( 10 × (35 − price) / 25 )
 + 0.4 × clamp( 10 × (0.90 − perGram)
 / 0.65 )
 where perGram = price / protein_g
 
Rewards a cheap meal and good protein value together. Ignores portion size, which is its main flaw.
 

 
 Before you trust a number
 

## How much of this is measured
 
 
7 of the 60 entries are built from a chain's own published nutrition documents — 2 more than the first edition claimed, because Chopt and Sweetgreen both publish nutrition the first edition did not consult. 2 are partial. The other 51 are reasoned from how the dish is put together, and that reasoning leaves fingerprints: 46 of them land on a round multiple of 50 mg of sodium and 44 on a multiple of 20 calories. Real nutrition panels do not do that. The published entries here read 380, 400, 415, 542, 575, 775 and 880 calories.
 
The gap between the tiers is not small, and it is the most important caveat in this document:
 
 
| Confidence tier | Entries | Mean health | Mean sodium mg | Mean flavor | Mean price | 
| Published | 7 | 6.73 | 749 | 5.56 | $16.58 | 
| Partial | 2 | 5.61 | 1,048 | 5.15 | $13.42 | 
| Estimated | 51 | 5.84 | 1,278 | 6.91 | $19.38 | 
 
 

#### Read that table as a warning, not a result
 
Entries built from published data score 0.89 points higher on health and carry 529 mg less sodium than estimated ones. Some of that is real — the chains that publish nutrition are exactly the build-your-own bowl chains whose format permits a low-sodium build. But some of it is estimation, and an estimator who does not know a number reaches for a plausible restaurant figure, which for sodium is high. The safest reading is that the published entries are accurate and the estimated ones are directionally right and individually soft. Where a dish's rank matters to you, the entry tells you which kind it is.
 

 
 Against interest
 

## Where this model misfires
 
 
Every criterion in this document was checked against current dietary guidance and the primary literature. Five produce results their own author would struggle to defend. They are printed here because a ranking that never argues with itself is not worth reading, and because a reader who knows where a model is weak can still use it.
 
 

### The gut criterion gives fiber no weight at all
It scores live ferments at 60% and a count of plant types at 40%. Fiber contributes nothing, and across the set fiber and the gut score correlate at only +0.31. There is a narrow defence — a 17-week trial found a fermented-food diet raised microbiome diversity where a high-fiber diet did not — but that defence is about alpha diversity, not about gut health as a reader would understand the phrase, and the criterion is labelled for the reader. As built it is a live-culture detector wearing a gut-health label.
Wastyk et al., Cell, August 2021.

### The kidney criterion stops measuring at 2,000 mg
It runs linearly from 350 mg to 2,000 and then floors, so every dish past that point scores zero and none can be told apart. That sounds academic until you meet Chosun Ok. This edition corrected its sodium from 2,100 mg to about 4,000 mg, once the 1 lb kimchi side was counted at USDA’s figure of 498 mg per 100 g — roughly 2,300 mg from the kimchi alone, before the stew. Its overall score moved by 0.02. A dish carrying nearly twice a full day’s recommended sodium is scored as merely equal to the worst thing the criterion can express.
USDA FoodData Central, FNDDS 2021–2023, food code 75502520.

### Sugar is scored as total, not added
Current dietary guidance targets added and free sugars and explicitly exempts the sugars in fruit and plain milk. This criterion does not distinguish them, so a bowl of fruit and a honey vinaigrette are penalised identically. First Watch’s Tri-Fecta — eggs, avocado, seasonal fruit and whole-grain toast — takes a sugar score of 5.3 mostly on the fruit, where the same 12 g from a glaze would cost exactly as much.
Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2025–2030.

### The omega-3 ladder treats flaxseed as if it were fish
The inflammation criterion scores omega-3 on a single ladder that does not distinguish plant ALA from marine EPA and DHA. It should. A review of 13 randomised trials found high-dose flaxseed and echium oil produced no increase in the omega-3 index at all, against reliable increases from fish oil; conversion of ALA to EPA runs at a few percent and to DHA below one. Every entry here scored for flaxseed is scored generously.
Lane et al., Critical Reviews in Food Science and Nutrition, 2022.

### Cost knows nothing about how much food you get
It rewards a low price and good protein value and ignores portion size entirely. A 320-calorie dish and a 780-calorie dish at the same price and protein score identically, though only one of them is dinner. On a list whose portions range more than twofold, this is the criterion most likely to mislead.
 
 

#### And what held up
 
Two of the model’s more contestable calls survived the check. Weighting the liver criterion on saturated fat rather than sugar is right: in a head-to-head overfeeding trial, saturated fat raised liver fat substantially more than unsaturated fat or simple sugars did — though the same guidance also names excess fructose, so a small added-sugar term belongs in that criterion and is not there. And the rule that vinegar quick-pickles score zero is correct: quick-process pickles are brined briefly then covered in vinegar with no fermentation step at all, a real and frequently-missed distinction. Labneh, cacık and raita are confirmed live-culture items, conditional only on the yogurt not having been heat-treated after culturing — which no menu will tell you.

### Telba shiro's ground flaxseed is 'a genuine plant omega-3 source', scored as omega-3 in the inflammation criterion
 
First edition Scored as an omega-3 source alongside oily fish.
 
Flaxseed is a real source of ALA and not a functional source of EPA or DHA. A scoping review of 13 randomised trials found high-dose flaxseed and echium oil produced no increase in the omega-3 index, against reliable increases from fish oil; conversion of ALA to EPA runs at a few percent and to DHA below one percent. The model is treating several grams of ALA as interchangeable with about a gram of preformed EPA and DHA, and it should not.

 Method and provenance
 
 
 

#### What a score is
 
Each entry is one specific dish, ordered one specific way, and every figure describes that dish as built — not the restaurant, and not the menu item as it arrives by default. Where the build says to refuse something, the numbers assume you refused it.
 

#### Prices
 
DoorDash-listed, which run roughly 15–25% above in-store. Distances are straight-line from Research Triangle Park; an active storefront is not the same as delivery to a given address, which is computed per address at checkout.
 
 
 

#### What this is not
 
Not medical advice, and not a clinical instrument. The criteria are a defensible way to compare restaurant meals with each other; they are not a diagnosis and the anchor points are judgement calls, some of which are argued with above.
 

#### Reproducibility
 
The six computed criteria are printed as functions and the dataset behind this edition is machine-readable. Any score here can be recomputed, disagreed with, and refitted with different anchors.
 
 
 

 Second edition. First edition compiled 25 August 2026; this revision 28 August 2026. Menus and formulations change constantly and every figure here has a shelf life measured in months.
