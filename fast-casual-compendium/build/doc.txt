 What changed in this edition
 
- The numbers were checked, and some were wrong. Every nutrition figure the first edition attributed to a published document was re-read against that document. 13 entries had to be rescored, including two of the first edition's top five: El Cuscatleco from 2nd to 23rd and Sassool from 3rd to 42nd. The largest single move was Bul Box from 23rd to 82nd. Everything checked is listed in the verification log at the end, including what came back clean and what could not be settled.
- The front page is new. The first edition opened with four findings, three of which were trivia about a single restaurant each and the fourth about research method rather than food. They have been replaced with findings drawn from the whole set, led by what the data says about money.
- The two lists are now one. The first edition printed “The Thirty” and “The Next Thirty” as separate ranked lists, but the split tracked research depth rather than score: 27 entries in the second list outscored the weakest in the first. Everything is now ranked in one sequence.
- The scoring model is printed, not described. Every criterion that can be computed from the macros is given as an actual function, recovered by fitting the first edition's own published scores. Anyone can recompute anything here, including the parts they disagree with.
- The sample was too narrow. 18 of the original sixty entries were build-your-own bowls or poke — 30% of the set, and 4 of the top ten — in a state whose own barbecue did not appear at all. This edition adds 27 dishes from cuisines and formats the first edition skipped.

 Chapter one
 

## What money does and does not buy
 
 
Across all 60 dishes, the correlation between what a dish costs on DoorDash and how healthy it scores is +0.05. Squared, that is well under one percent of the variation: price tells you essentially nothing about whether a meal is good for you. Leave out any single dish and it never leaves the range -0.03 to +0.14, so it is not one outlier holding it down.
 
Money does buy something. The correlation between price and flavor is +0.41 — modest, but five times the size and in a direction you can feel. Expensive food in this set is more interesting to eat and no better for you. Everything in this chapter follows from those two numbers.

## Sodium is the currency
 
 
Sodium separates this list more than any other single number, and the useful way to hold it is not as a total but as a rate: how much sodium you are paying for each gram of protein you actually get. On that measure the spread across the set is more than twentyfold.
 
DICED delivers protein at 2.5 mg of sodium per gram. Bul Box charges 84.8 mg for the same gram. Both are on the same list, in the same city, at similar prices.

#### The counterintuitive part
 
Only 3 of the 10 swaps above raise the dish's score while cutting sodium. Look at what the rest give up: Namu's banchan plates save 600 mg and lose 26 g of protein. Southern Spice's idli-without-sambar saves 560 mg and drops the score by half a point. The reliable way to cut sodium at a restaurant is to eat less of the meal, which is a real option and a poor one. The exceptions — the ones where you cut salt without cutting food — are almost all sauce refusals, and they are listed above under “where the salt actually is.”
 

 
 Chapter three
 

## What flavor actually costs
 
 
The first edition argued that cuisines built on char, acid and aromatics keep their flavor when you strip out salt and fat, while cuisines built on sauce, cheese and frying do not. The first half of that is right and the second half is aimed at the wrong nutrient.
 
Take the 22 dishes scoring 7.5 or better on flavor and the 14 scoring 5.5 or worse. The flavorful group carries +572 mg more sodium, at t = 4.5. It also carries +2.0 g more saturated fat, at t = 2.4, which is on the edge of meaning nothing at all.
 
The sodium gap is not one dish doing the work. Leave any single dish out and it stays between 520 and 655 mg, never falling below t = 4.1; on medians rather than means the gap is +650 mg.
 
So flavor in this set is bought mainly with salt, not with fat, and the first edition was policing the wrong nutrient. That is also why there is no health-flavor tradeoff between individual dishes — the correlation is −0.12, nil. The tradeoff is not a property of a dish. It is a property of a kitchen's whole seasoning strategy, and it shows up only when you group by cuisine.

#### Read the last two columns together
 
Salt is what flavor costs. It is not the only way a cuisine loses the health column. Korean, Sichuan and Vietnamese kitchens sit high on flavor and low on health entirely on sodium — their saturated fat is unremarkable. Caribbean and Mexican kitchens lose it a different way, on 11.0 g and 10.7 g of saturated fat against a set median near 5.0, and they do not get leading flavor scores in exchange. Those are the two failure modes, and only one of them buys you anything.
 

 
 

#### Why the set-level correlation is nearly zero
 
It is not that health and flavor are unrelated. It is that two criteria pull in opposite directions and very nearly cancel. Decompose the covariance and kidney contributes −0.31 while gut contributes +0.28 — the salty, fermented kitchens lose on one exactly as fast as they win on the other. Take the kidney criterion out and health correlates +0.12 with flavor; take gut out instead and it goes to −0.31. The flat headline number is an artifact of the model's own weighting, not a fact about food.
 
The reason those two criteria fight is physical rather than statistical: almost every live ferment a restaurant can sell you is preserved in salt. Ask this list for a dish scoring 7 or better on both gut and kidney and it returns 0 of 60. Only Goorsha clears 6 on both, at gut 6.2 and kidney 7.0. Yogurt is the one escape route in this data — labneh, cacık and raita carry live cultures without a brine — which is why the Levantine and Turkish entries come closest.
 

 

### The market rate for a flavor point
 
Regress flavor on sodium across all 60 dishes and the exchange rate is 436 mg per point (r = +0.60). That is the going price of taste on this list. 11 dishes beat it by more than a full point — and 9 of those 11 are grilled. This is the first edition's thesis, stated as a number rather than an assertion: char, acid and aromatics are how a kitchen buys flavor without paying in salt.

### Why each one is here
 Turkish

### Talullas — Balık Şiş
The second Turkish entry earns its place by not repeating the first: no bulgur, no pide, no rice — a char-grilled wild salmon şiş that arrives on arugula and nothing else. It is the only plate in this sweep with no starch on it at all. The haydari follows Turkish mezze logic and does the same job the compendium's cacık does at Bosphorus.
Charcoal on oily fish is the best argument in Turkish grilling: skin blisters, the fat renders into the char. Arugula is peppery and bitter, lemon is aggressive, the strained yogurt is thick and garlicky. Almost nothing is declined except the bread basket. The gap is fibre and vegetables — this plate is fish, leaves and yogurt.
Order it: Balık Şiş · char-grilled wild salmon with arugula salad · add Haydari ($8, cream of yogurt with dill and garlic) · REFUSE the bread · lemon over all of itPeruvian

### Alpaca Peruvian Charcoal Chicken — ¼ Chicken w/ White Combo
The sides are the whole story: two are included, and black beans and house salad sit in the same price tier as fries and white rice. Take them and a quarter of charcoal white meat delivers 53 grams of protein for $11.29 — 21 cents a gram, among the three cheapest in the book. The soy-heavy brine is what keeps sodium near 1,300.
Real charcoal does real work: the skin browns properly, the ají panca and cumin in the overnight brine push through, and a low soy savour sits underneath. What you surrender by refusing the ají verde is most of the richness — it is mayonnaise with green chile, and it is worth roughly a point of flavour against a point of health.
Order it: ¼ Chicken w/ White Combo (breast & wing) · black beans · house salad, dressing on the side and mostly unused · REFUSE the hand-cut fries and the white rice · REFUSE the yellow sauce outright; one ounce of green sauce is a fair trade but costs about two tenths of a pointCuban

### Carmen's Cuban Cafe & Lounge — Ceviche Salad
Twenty years in Morrisville, and the lean order is not the ropa vieja — it is the ceviche, moved onto the house salad. Every entree here arrives with three sides, and that structure is what sinks the braises: rice, beans and maduros add four hundred calories before the meat does anything. The salad version skips all of it.
Citrus cure, raw onion, a little cilantro, and whatever the vinaigrette brings. It is the quietest dish in this batch — no char, no ferment, almost no fat — and the Cuban kitchen's actual weapons, mojo and sofrito and slow-roast pork, are all sitting on the plates you just declined. Bright, clean, faintly under-seasoned.
Order it: Ceviche Salad · vinaigrette on the side and about half of it used · REFUSE the Cuban bread · do NOT convert it into an entree plate — the three included sides are what ruin every other dish here · add black beans (+$3.95) only if you need the meal larger, and know it costs about 450 mg of sodiumPersian

### Flame Kabob — Chicken Breast (Boneless) kabob
The compendium had no Persian entry and this is why that was a mistake: the menu itself offers salad in place of the chelo, so the single best move in Persian food is a printed option rather than a negotiation. One breast skewer carries 46 g of protein off a charcoal grill, and the $7.99 Cucumber Yogurt is a live-culture bowl, not a two-ounce ramekin.
Charcoal does the work — lemon-and-onion marinade, real blistering, saffron and turmeric underneath. The mast-o-khiar brings tang, mint and dill. What you give up is the chelo itself, the buttered saffron rice that is half the point of a chelo-kabob, and breast is the driest skewer they grill; koobideh survives the strip better.
Order it: Chicken Breast (Boneless) kabob · take the SALAD, not the white rice — the menu offers the swap · add Cucumber Yogurt (mast-o-khiar, $7.99) · grilled tomato mashed over the meat · REFUSE the tandoori bread and REFUSE the ranchSalvadoran

### Pupuseria Doña Laura — Pechuga a la Planca
Three Durham storefronts, and curtido comes with every pupusa order — but only with pupusas, which is the finding. Order the grilled breast and you get 54 grams of protein at 470 calories and lose the ferment entirely. I could not establish whether their curtido is a true ferment or a same-day vinegar slaw, so it is scored at zero here.
Plancha sear, garlic, a squeeze of lime, and a plain cabbage-and-tomato salad. Honest but thin — this is a kitchen whose best work is masa and curtido, and the grilled-breast plate is what remains once you route around both. Half the rice goes back in the box and you lose nothing you wanted.
Order it: Pechuga a la plancha · the vegetable salad it comes with · half the rice left in the box · ask them to add a cup of curtido on the side — unconfirmed whether they will sell it apart from a pupusa order, and this entry is scored as if they will not · REFUSE the tajadas and the cremaThai/Isaan

### Twisted Noodles — SL6. Grilled Nam-Tok Salad
The compendium's one Thai entry was a curry. Nam tok is the other Thailand — Isaan, grilled, herb-forward, dressed rather than sauced. Twisted Noodle grills the beef, showers it with toasted rice powder and chili-lime, and sends no rice with it, so forty grams of protein arrives at just over 400 calories. The fish sauce is what keeps it off the podium.
Nothing is stripped, so everything lands: real grill char on the beef, lime at full strength, mint and cilantro and shallot stacked high, and toasted rice powder giving both nuttiness and grit. Fish sauce supplies the fermented floor under all of it. This is the most complete flavor architecture in the sweep after the tea leaf salad, and it costs seventeen dollars.
Order it: SL6 with beef rather than pork · ask for it Thai hot and for extra toasted rice powder · dressed as served, since nam tok is tossed in the kitchen and asking for it on the side wrecks it · refuse the side of steamed sticky rice and refuse the peanut-ginger dressing they offer with the house saladJamaican

### Mum's Jamaican Restaurant — Steam Fish (large)
Ordered steamed rather than brown-stewed, this beats the compendium's three existing Caribbean entries by a wide margin. A whole snapper in a thyme-and-pimento broth is fifty-seven grams of protein for well under six hundred calories. The butter in the broth is the only meaningful fat; skipping the rice and the water crackers does the rest.
Everything comes from the broth: thyme, pimento, scallion and a whole scotch bonnet bobbing in it, with okra thickening as it sits. There is no char and almost no acid, which is exactly why it scores where it does. Losing the crackers that normally soak up that broth costs a little texture.
Order it: "Brown Stew Fish & Steam Fish" ordered STEAMED, large · add Steam Vegetables ($4.49) · no rice and peas, no white rice, no festival, no fried dumpling · leave the water crackers in the bagChinese-American

### Golden Dragon — D6. Steamed Shrimp with Mixed Vegetables Diet
Every American Chinese takeout prints a Diet Menu and nobody orders from it. Golden Dragon's carries its own instruction — steamed with sauce on the side and no oil — which is the entire nutritional argument in eight words. A quart of shrimp and mixed vegetables with the wok taken out of the equation is the cleanest order within a mile of NC State.
There is almost none, and that is the honest verdict. Steam extracts nothing; the brown sauce in its plastic cup carries the only soy depth and the only garlic. What survives is texture — snappy shrimp, vegetables still squeaking — and the fact that you meter the sauce yourself. It is a rebuke to the wok, not a dish.
Order it: D6 from the Diet Menu · it arrives steamed with the sauce cup on the side, which is how the menu itself specifies it · use half the sauce cup, no more · eat about half the white rice and leave the rest · refuse the fried noodles, the duck sauce and the soy packets that come in the bagGreek

### Kipos Greek Taverna — Souvlaki, chicken
A real taverna with a live wood fire, not a bowl chain, and the archetype arrives assembled: grilled whole-muscle chicken, a live-yogurt tzatziki that is part of the dish rather than an upcharge, and a tomato-cucumber salad. The lemon potatoes are what hold the score down — whole potato, but a lot of it, in a lot of olive oil.
Wood fire, oregano, garlic and enough lemon that acid runs through every component. The tzatziki is properly sour and thick. Nothing here has been stripped except the pita, which is why it reads richer than the leaner plates around it; the only compromise is that the potatoes soak up most of the olive oil on the plate.
Order it: SOUVLAKI, chicken · eat the tzatziki, all of it · REFUSE the pita · lemon potatoes as they come, they are whole potato in olive oil · extra lemonHalal cart

### The Halal Guys — Regular Chicken Platter
The compendium had no halal cart, and the two questions worth asking both have answers here. Yes, it can be had over lettuce — rice out, double lettuce in, no upcharge. And the white sauce costs nothing at the register and 600 kcal with 56 g of fat in the box, because a regular platter ships with two packets. Ordered this way it is 56 g of protein for 446.
This is the entry that pays for its health score in flavor. The chicken is genuinely spiced — coriander, turmeric, oregano — and the hot sauce is sharp. But the white sauce is the thing people cross town for, and refusing it plus the rice leaves chopped griddled chicken on lettuce. Flat-top browning, no live char, nothing fermented anywhere on the plate.

### Road To Myanmar Asian Cuisine — Laphet Thoke (Green tea Salad)
Laphet is not a garnish or a vinegar trick: tea leaf packed and lacto-fermented for months, and the only genuinely live ferment in this entire sweep. Around it sit four fried legumes and seeds doing the protein work with no grain of rice anywhere. Thirty-one grams of protein and fourteen of fiber, out of a salad, on Franklin Street, and the compendium missed it.
All of it survives, because there is nothing to strip. The tea leaves are sour, tannic and faintly funky; lime and green chili sharpen them; fried split peas, broad beans, peanuts and sesame supply four separate crunches and the oil to carry everything else. Only char is absent. This is the highest flavor score in the sweep and it is not close.

### Sam Jones BBQ — Smoked Turkey (Smokehouse Plate)
Sliced turkey breast is the least-ordered thing on a whole-hog board and by a distance the leanest: 55 g of protein for 462 calories and under 5 g of saturated fat. Collards and the house salad carry the plants. The sweet potato muffin is where a plate like this usually goes wrong, so it gets declined. Seasoned turkey plus pork-cooked greens still put sodium near 1,750 mg.
This is the trade the compendium keeps having to make explicit. Oak smoke reaches the breast, but there is no bark and no fat to carry it, so all the acid has to be poured on from the vinegar bottle. Declining the sweet potato muffin removes the one genuinely delicious thing on the plate.
Order it: Smoked Turkey plate · two sides: Collard Greens + House Salad, dressing on the side and half used · vinegar sauce applied from the table · REFUSE the sweet potato muffin, cornbread and toast, and the mac 'n cheese and sweet slawNorth Carolina barbecue

### Lawrence Barbecue — Smoked Party Wings, 10 pc
The answer to the wings question is no. Lawrence smokes them and then fries them, which the menu states plainly, so the smoked wing is not an alternative to the fried one — it is a fried one with better flavor. Choosing the dry rub over hot honey keeps sugar near 3 g. There is no plant on the plate, and the scores say so.
Best-tasting thing in this sweep and the worst-scoring on nearly everything else. Oak smoke goes in before the fryer, so you get bark and crackle at once, and the all-purpose rub is aromatic without leaning on sugar. The house pickles supply the only acid, and being vinegar quick-pickles they earn no ferment credit.
Order it: Smoked Party Wings, 10 pc · AP Dry Rub (the only sugar-light option on the sauce board) · keep the house pickles · REFUSE the Martin's rolls, and refuse Helen's Hot Honey, Beef Fat Caramel, Roasted Garlic BBQ and Chamoy-TajinBrazilian

### Boteco do Brazil — House Salad with Sliced Picanha
The answer to whether anyone sells the grilled protein without the buffet: Boteco lets you add sliced picanha to the house salad for nine dollars and it arrives as a real plate, not a garnish. The fat cap is why saturated fat sits at twelve grams; the peas, corn and balsamic are why sugar reaches thirteen.

### Hady's Restaurant International Cuisine — Beef Suya
Hady's grills suya to order on skewers and lets you take a vegetable instead of rice — the fork in the menu that decides everything here. Seven ounces of beef under a peanut-and-yaji crust carries sixty-odd grams of protein at zero refined carbohydrate. The bouillon in the rub and the braised collards are what push sodium past sixteen hundred.
Almost all of it is dry heat and yaji: ground peanut, ginger, clove and cayenne baked onto the meat by open flame. Nothing is lost by refusing the rice, because suya was never carried by starch — it is finger food. What you give up is only the fried plantain's sweetness against the pepper.

### Chop Naija Kitchen — Vegetable Soup
The soup is the leafy end of the Nigerian menu and the swallow is the calorie problem, so this is scored without pounded yam. Greens, crayfish and assorted protein give an unusual protein density; two tablespoons of palm oil across a pint deliver seventeen grams of saturated fat, and that one ingredient is the entire ceiling on this dish.
Palm oil, fermented locust bean and dried crayfish do the work — a deep, funky, red-orange base that needs no browning at all. Scotch bonnet carries it. Refusing the pounded yam costs real texture, because the soup is built to be scooped rather than spooned; eaten alone it reads very rich and slightly one-note.

### Al's Burger Shack — Connie
The grilled chicken sandwich the reader was promised might surprise. ### The Pit Authentic Barbecue — Chopped BBQ (barbecue plate)

### Zweli's Ekhaya — Berbere Half Chicken
The only Zimbabwean kitchen in the Triangle, and the one African entry here that grills rather than stews. Skin and the hot-honey finish are what stop saturated fat and sugar from being low.
Berbere is a fifteen-spice wall — fenugreek, cardamom, ajwain, chili — and the grill sets it hard. The slaw's vinegar cuts the skin. Holding back most of the hot honey is the right call nutritionally and a genuine loss on the plate, because that sweet-heat glaze is the dish's whole argument.

### Another Broken Egg Cafe — Veggie Delight Omelette
The one breakfast in this sweep that publishes its numbers, and they are better than the format deserves: 630 milligrams of sodium is the lowest figure on this page, in a category otherwise built on bacon, biscuit and sausage gravy — their own Southern Breakfast Bowl carries 4,500. Thirty-one grams of protein from eggs alone, before any side.
Sautéed vegetables, melted cheese, folded egg: competent and completely unassertive. There is no acid, no char worth the name, and no ferment beyond the cheese. ### Grand Malabar Indian Cuisine — Kappa & Meen Curry
Kerala cooking, not a curry-house menu with a fish add-on. Malabar meen curry is soured with kudampuli rather than enriched with cream, and the fish portion is a real one, which makes this the only genuine omega-3 plate the whole sweep turned up. The cost is coconut oil: it goes into the kappa and again into the tempering.
Curry leaves popped in coconut oil, shallot, fenugreek, mustard seed, and kudampuli's dark resinous sourness — an aromatic ceiling almost nothing else in this guide reaches. No char and no ferment at all. The kappa is deliberately plain, there to absorb gravy, so the texture score comes from flaking fish rather than from contrast.

### Dim Sum House — 37. Slice Fish Congee

### The Fiction Kitchen — Eastern NC Style BBQ Sandwich

### Let's Eat Soul Food — Baked Chicken Wings Combo with 2 Sides
The baked wing is the rare non-fried order at a soul-food counter and it is worth knowing about. But baking does not remove the skin fat, and seasoning salt plus pork-cooked collards and cabbage push sodium past 2,100 mg — enough to zero the kidney score outright. That is the honest answer to whether the vegetable sides rescue a soul-food plate. They do not.

 The model
 

## Nine criteria, written out
 
 
The first edition described its criteria in prose. This one prints them as functions, because a scoring model you cannot recompute is an opinion wearing a number's clothes.
 
Six of the nine are arithmetic on the six macros and the price, and are reproduced here exactly: feeding the first edition's own published macros back through them returns every one of its 60 printed overall scores to within 0.10, with a mean deviation of 0.023. Three — gut, energy and inflammation — are human judgements on a rubric, and no formula recovers them. Where a shape can be inferred from the pattern of scores it is given with an approximation sign; where it cannot, the criterion is simply marked as judged.
 
Composite: health = (KID + LIV + MUS + GUT + ENE + INF + 0.7×SUG) / 6.7, then overall = (health×6.7 + FLAVOR×0.5 + COST×0.5) / 7.7.
 
The first edition wrote that flavor and cost at half weight “reorder the middle of the list without overturning it.” That is checkable, and it holds. Multiply each column's weight by the range it actually spans across these 60 dishes and you get the most it could ever move an overall score: KID 1.26, GUT 1.22, INF 1.22, MUS 1.13, LIV 1.12, ENE 0.97, SUG 0.91, cost 0.58, flavor 0.42. Flavor and cost are the two smallest levers in the model by some distance.
 
 
 

### Kidney×1.0
 
Sodium alone.
 
KID = clamp( 10 × (2000 − sodium_mg) / 1650 )
 
Full marks at 350 mg, zero at 2,000. Linear between, which means a dish at 2,100 mg and one at 3,000 mg both score zero.
 
 

### Liver×1.0
 
Saturated fat 65%, calorie load 35%.
 
LIV = 0.65 × clamp( 10 × (16.5 − satfat_g) / 14.5 )
 + 0.35 × clamp( 10 × (1000 − cal) / 500 )
 
The only criterion whose anchors had to be fitted rather than read off. Reproduces the published scores with a mean deviation of 0.13.
 
 

### Muscle×1.0
 
Protein density 70%, absolute protein 30%.
 
MUS = 0.7 × clamp( 10 × (perKcal − 2) / 7 )
 + 0.3 × clamp( 10 × (protein_g − 20) / 25 )
 where perKcal = protein_g / (cal/100)
 
Rewards protein per calorie first, so a light dish with 30 g beats a heavy one with 40.
 
 

### Gut×1.0
 
Live ferments 60%, plant-type count 40%.
 
GUT ≈ 0.6 × (live-ferment ladder, 0–4)
 + 0.4 × (plant types, capped at 19)
 
A judgement, not a calculation — the ladder above is the smallest one consistent with all 60 published scores, not a formula the first edition states. Vinegar pickles score zero. Fiber carries no weight at all — see the audit below. Judged, not computed.
 
 

### Energy×1.0
 
Refined-carb load, fiber, protein presence.
 
ENE — rubric, 0–10
 
A glycemic proxy assembled by hand. It correlates with fiber at +0.38, which is the closest thing to a check available on it. Judged, not computed.
 
 

### Inflammation×1.0
 
Omega-3 60%, plant and herb diversity 40%.
 
INF ≈ 0.6 × (omega-3 ladder, 0–3)
 + 0.4 × (plant and herb diversity, 0–7)
 
Inferred, like the gut ladder, rather than recovered exactly. The omega-3 ladder does not distinguish plant ALA from marine EPA and DHA, which overstates flaxseed — see the audit. Judged, not computed.
 
 

### Sugar×0.7
 
Total sugars.
 
SUG = clamp( 10 × (22 − sugar_g) / 19 )
 
Full marks at 3 g, zero at 22. Uses total sugars, so fruit and plain dairy are penalised exactly like added sugar.
 
 

### Flavor×0.5
 
Six components, minus what the health edit removes.
 
FLAVOR = ( char 0–3 + acid 0–2
 + ferment 0–2 + aromatics 0–3
 + richness 0–2 + texture 0–2
 − strip penalty 0–3 ) / 14 × 10
 
Half-points allowed. Not a taste rating: it measures where a dish's intensity comes from and how much survives ordering it the healthy way. Judged, not computed.
 
 

### Cost×0.5
 
Listed price 60%, price per gram of protein 40%.
 
COST = 0.6 × clamp( 10 × (35 − price) / 25 )
 + 0.4 × clamp( 10 × (0.90 − perGram)
 / 0.65 )
 where perGram = price / protein_g
 
Rewards a cheap meal and good protein value together. Ignores portion size, which is its main flaw.
 

 
 Before you trust a number
 

## How much of this is measured
 
 
7 of the 60 entries are built from a chain’s own published nutrition documents — 2 more than the first edition claimed, because Chopt and Sweetgreen both publish figures it never consulted. 2 are partial. The other 51 are reasoned from how the dish is put together, and that reasoning leaves fingerprints: 46 of them land on a round multiple of 50 mg of sodium and 42 on a multiple of 20 calories. Real nutrition panels do not do that. The published entries here read 380, 400, 415, 542, 575, 775 and 880 calories.
 
 
| Confidence tier | Entries | Mean health | Mean sodium mg | Mean flavor | Mean price | 
| Published | 7 | 6.73 | 749 | 5.56 | $16.58 | 
| Partial | 2 | 5.61 | 1,048 | 5.15 | $13.42 | 
| Estimated | 51 | 5.79 | 1,231 | 6.91 | $19.34 | 
 
 

#### The gap in that table is not what it looks like
 
Read naively, published entries carry 482 mg less sodium than estimated ones and score 1.35 lower on flavor. That reads like an estimator who reaches for a high, dull number when they do not know one. It is not. Every one of the 9 entries with published or partial data is an assembled bowl or a plate of raw fish. Not one of the 36 dishes here that meets heat — grilled, stewed or steamed — rests on a published number. Nobody publishes nutrition for food cooked to order over fire, so the published tier and the bowl-chain tier are the same tier.
 
Control for that and the apparent bias mostly disappears. Compare only the assembled and raw dishes with each other — 9 verified against 15 estimated, same format — and the flavor gap collapses to +0.01 and the sodium gap actually reverses to -136 mg. The estimator was not systematically pessimistic. The sample was systematically narrow.
 
Which leaves the caveat in a more useful place. Trust the ranking’s treatment of bowls, salads and raw fish, because that is where the real numbers are. Treat every grilled, stewed and steamed entry as a construction estimate that no restaurant has confirmed — and note that this edition’s largest corrections, Sassool and El Cuscatleco, both came from exactly that group.
 

 
 Against interest
 

## Where this model misfires
 
 
Every criterion in this document was checked against current dietary guidance and the primary literature. Five produce results their own author would struggle to defend. They are printed here because a ranking that never argues with itself is not worth reading, and because a reader who knows where a model is weak can still use it.
 
 

### The gut criterion gives fiber no weight at all
It scores live ferments at 60% and a count of plant types at 40%. Fiber contributes nothing, and across the set fiber and the gut score correlate at only +0.30. There is a narrow defence — a 17-week trial found a fermented-food diet raised microbiome diversity where a high-fiber diet did not — but that defence is about alpha diversity, not about gut health as a reader would understand the phrase, and the criterion is labelled for the reader. As built it is a live-culture detector wearing a gut-health label.
Wastyk et al., Cell, August 2021.

One line of an unstated rubric is worth more to a dish’s position than most of the arithmetic above it.

### The kidney criterion stops measuring at 2,000 mg
It runs linearly from 350 mg to 2,000 and then floors, so every dish past that point scores zero and none can be told apart. This edition produced its own demonstration. Chosun Ok’s stew was corrected twice: first up to about 4,000 mg, once the 1 lb kimchi side was counted at USDA’s 498 mg per 100 g, and then back down to 1,700 mg when the kimchi side turned out not to exist on the menu at all. The first correction nearly doubled the dish’s sodium and the kidney criterion did not move at all — it was already at zero. Only the second correction, which brought the figure back under the ceiling, changed anything. A scale that cannot tell 2,100 mg from 4,000 is not measuring the thing it is named for at the end of its range that matters most.
USDA FoodData Central, FNDDS 2021–2023, food code 75502520; order.chosunoknc.com.

### Sugar is scored as total, not added
Current dietary guidance targets added and free sugars and explicitly exempts the sugars in fruit and plain milk. This criterion does not distinguish them, so a bowl of fruit and a honey vinaigrette are penalised identically. First Watch’s Tri-Fecta — eggs, avocado, seasonal fruit and whole-grain toast — takes a sugar score of 5.3 mostly on the fruit, where the same 12 g from a glaze would cost exactly as much.
Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2025–2030.

### The omega-3 ladder treats flaxseed as if it were fish
The inflammation criterion scores omega-3 on a single ladder that does not distinguish plant ALA from marine EPA and DHA. It should. A review of 13 randomised trials found high-dose flaxseed and echium oil produced no increase in the omega-3 index at all, against reliable increases from fish oil; conversion of ALA to EPA runs at a few percent and to DHA below one. Every entry here scored for flaxseed is scored generously.
Lane et al., Critical Reviews in Food Science and Nutrition, 2022.

### Cost knows nothing about how much food you get
It rewards a low price and good protein value and ignores portion size entirely. A 320-calorie dish and a 780-calorie dish at the same price and protein score identically, though only one of them is dinner. On a list whose portions range more than twofold, this is the criterion most likely to mislead.
 
 

#### And what held up
 
Two of the model’s more contestable calls survived the check. Weighting the liver criterion on saturated fat rather than sugar is right: in a head-to-head overfeeding trial, saturated fat raised liver fat substantially more than unsaturated fat or simple sugars did — though the same guidance also names excess fructose, so a small added-sugar term belongs in that criterion and is not there. And the rule that vinegar quick-pickles score zero is correct: quick-process pickles are brined briefly then covered in vinegar with no fermentation step at all, a real and frequently-missed distinction. Labneh, cacık and raita are confirmed live-culture items, conditional only on the yogurt not having been heat-treated after culturing — which no menu will tell you.

## What was wrong
 
 
Every nutrition figure the first edition attributed to a published document was re-read against that document, and the restaurants were spot-checked for whether they exist and are trading. 36 claims came back wrong, 13 of them badly enough to change a score. 7 could not be settled either way and are printed as open rather than quietly dropped.
 
Two of the corrections move an entry a long way. Sassool was ranked third on figures its own published nutrition sheet does not support. Bul Box was scored on sodium inferred from construction because its nutrition page was returning an error; the page works now, and the real number is more than double.

 CorrectedAbol Ethiopian
 

### Telba shiro's ground flaxseed is 'a genuine plant omega-3 source', scored as omega-3 in the inflammation criterion
 
First edition Scored as an omega-3 source alongside oily fish.
 
Flaxseed is a real source of ALA and not a functional source of EPA or DHA. A scoping review of 13 randomised trials found high-dose flaxseed and echium oil produced no increase in the omega-3 index, against reliable increases from fish oil; conversion of ALA to EPA runs at a few percent and to DHA below one percent. The model is treating several grams of ALA as interchangeable with about a gram of preformed EPA and DHA, and it should not.
 
 
Lane et al., Critical Reviews in Food Science and Nutrition, 2022 (PMID 33576691).

### Salmon is 'one of few foods with trial evidence for reducing liver fat'
 
First edition Given as the reason the salmon build scores well on the liver criterion.
 
No randomised trial of salmon with liver fat as an outcome could be found. The 13 indexed salmon-intervention trials cover vitamin D status, cognition, ulcerative colitis and pollutant kinetics. The defensible version is that oily fish and long-chain omega-3s have trial evidence for improving some liver markers, with the effect on directly measured liver fat inconsistent across meta-analyses.

 CorrectedM Sushi
 

### Salmon belly and chutoro 'carry several times the EPA and DHA of lean loin'
 
First edition Given as the reason to order the oily cuts.
 
Half right, and the right half is understated. Japan's standard food composition tables put fatty bluefin tuna at 4,600 mg of EPA plus DHA per 100 g against akami's roughly 150 - about thirty times, not several. Salmon does not work the same way: belly flap runs 18.5% lipid against 2.4% in the tail, so it is modestly richer in absolute omega-3 but slightly poorer as a proportion of its fatty acids. The advice is right for the tuna and overstated for the salmon.
 
 
MEXT Standard Tables of Food Composition, 8th edition; Katikou, Hughes & Robb, Aquaculture 202 (2001).

 Method and provenance
 
 
 

#### What a score is
 
Each entry is one specific dish, ordered one specific way, and every figure describes that dish as built — not the restaurant, and not the menu item as it arrives by default. Where the build says to refuse something, the numbers assume you refused it.
 

#### Prices
 
DoorDash-listed. Published measurements put the delivery-app premium at roughly 10–20% above in-store, averaging about 15% — the first edition said 15–25%, which is high at both ends. Every cost score here is computed on the delivery price, so the cost column is systematically harsher than an in-store comparison would be.
 

#### Storefronts
 
57 of the 60 entries print a DoorDash store id; three print a brand-level business id or the word “chain”. Thirty-five restaurants were independently spot-checked for this edition and all thirty-five exist and appear to be trading, with 29 of the printed ids matched to the right restaurant and address. Distances are straight-line from Research Triangle Park, and an active storefront is not the same as delivery to a given address — radius is computed per address at checkout.
 
 
 

#### What this is not
 
Not medical advice, and not a clinical instrument. The criteria are a defensible way to compare restaurant meals with each other; they are not a diagnosis and the anchor points are judgement calls, some of which are argued with above.
 

#### Reproducibility
 
The six computed criteria are printed as functions and the dataset behind this edition is machine-readable. Any score here can be recomputed, disagreed with, and refitted with different anchors.
