# Topic: household budgeting (self-study variation)

## SOURCE (give this to the assistant)

A household budget is a plan that allocates expected income to expected
expenses over a period, usually a month. Expenses are commonly split into
fixed costs, which stay the same each period (rent, insurance, loan
payments), and variable costs, which change with usage or choices
(groceries, fuel, entertainment). A budget is balanced when planned expenses
plus planned savings do not exceed expected income. Savings set aside for
irregular but predictable expenses — annual insurance premiums, car
maintenance, holiday gifts — are called sinking funds; without them, these
expenses appear as surprises even though they are foreseeable. An emergency
fund is different: it covers genuinely unpredictable events such as job loss
or urgent repairs, and a common guideline is three to six months of
essential expenses. Guidelines such as allocating 50% of income to needs,
30% to wants, and 20% to savings are starting points, not rules; households
with high fixed costs or irregular income must adapt the ratios. A budget
based on estimated numbers drifts from reality, so spending should be
reviewed against the plan regularly, and the plan revised when income or
circumstances change.

## SAMPLE OUTPUT (what a good result looks like)

### The main idea
A budget is a plan made **before** the money is spent: you decide where your
income should go each month, instead of finding out afterwards where it went.

### Two kinds of spending
Some costs are the same every month — rent, insurance, loan payments. These
are called **fixed costs**. Other costs change depending on what you do —
food, fuel, fun. These are **variable costs**. A budget lists both and adds
planned savings. The plan works when all of it together is not more than the
money coming in.

### Saving for things you can see coming
Some expenses do not happen every month but are not surprises either: a
yearly insurance bill, car repairs, holiday gifts. Money set aside a little
at a time for these is called a **sinking fund**. Without one, a bill you
could have predicted feels like an emergency.

### Saving for things you cannot see coming
A real **emergency fund** is for events you cannot predict — losing a job,
an urgent repair. A common guideline is enough to cover three to six months
of essential costs. Guideline, not rule: the same is true of the well-known
50/30/20 split (needs/wants/savings). If your rent is high or your income
changes month to month, your numbers will look different — and that is fine.

An analogy: a budget is like a shopping list. You write it before entering
the store, so the store does not decide for you. But the analogy stops
there — a shopping list is finished after one trip, while a budget must be
**checked and revised regularly**, because it is built on estimates that
drift away from real life.
